\chapter{Historical Perspective}\label{front:historical}

Annual conventions became a feature of model rocketry in 1966 when the Steel City Section of the
National Association of Rocketry held the first Model Rocketry Convention in Pittsburgh,
Pennsylvania. The Massachusetts Institute of Technology Model Rocket Society (MITMRS) held the first
MIT Model Rocketry Convention, which focused on research promoting the technical advancement of the
hobby, in 1968.

The idea for \emph{Topics in Advanced Model Rocketry} originated when The MIT Press became aware that
the MITMRS was planning a convention that would include the presentation of technical papers. The
Press' Editor approached MITMRS founding member George Caporaso with a proposal to publish the
presented papers as a bound volume. The authors welcomed the opportunity for publication, and after
the convention we expanded three of our papers by incorporating additional material. These became
Chapters~\ref{ch2}, \ref{ch3}, and~\ref{ch4} of \emph{Topics} and were supplemented by a new
introductory Chapter~\ref{ch1}.

The MIT Press published \emph{Topics in Advanced Model Rocketry} as a hardcover volume in 1973.
Interest in the book continued after the original press run was exhausted in 1980. All rights were
transferred from the Press to the authors in 1986. In 2003, the Press republished \emph{Topics} in
paperback as part of the Press' print-on-demand ``Classic'' series. The Press continued to make
\emph{Topics} available as a Classic until 2017. NAR member Christopher Feyerchak digitized
\emph{Topics} in 2025. The authors, with the blessing of The MIT Press, dedicated the original and
digitized works to the public domain in 2025.

To bring \emph{Topics in Advanced Model Rocketry} to publication as quickly and inexpensively as
possible, The MIT Press did not follow the normal procedure of typesetting the book and providing the
authors with galley proofs to proofread prior to publication. Instead, the Press created photo-offset
plates directly from the authors' typescript, ink drawings, and photographs and published the book
without proofreading, resulting in several errors in the original text. The authors identified some
of the errors immediately and prepared an errata page to be furnished with each copy. Readers
reported others to us over the years, and we prepared the corrections which are included in the
\hyperref[supp]{supplement} to the digital edition.

When \emph{Topics} was first published we were working only with small model rockets of 454 grams or
less liftoff mass using motors containing no more than 113 grams of solid propellant. The first model
rocket motors using ammonium perchlorate composite propellant with a specific impulse of 170 seconds
had just been introduced, but the vast majority of our motors still used black powder propellant of
about 80 seconds specific impulse. Model rocket motor total impulse classes did not extend beyond F
(80 newton-seconds maximum). Our rockets attained altitudes of only a few thousand meters, and those
altitudes were verified by law-of-sines calculations using azimuth and elevation angles measured by
optical theodolites. Radio transmitters had been developed for model rockets several years earlier,
but they still used discrete components and were bulky by modern standards. Model rocket instruments
and telemetry were in their infancy, and model-rocket-mounted photography and cinematography used
analog film. The earliest practical radio-controlled boost gliders using digital proportional rudder
control were just being developed. The computers we used to analyze model rocket flight filled rooms.
They used magnetic core memory banks and large sealed Winchester disks, read their program
instructions and input data from patterns of holes in Hollerith cards that had to be punched using
large keypunch machines, and provided results by printing them on fanfold paper with chain printers
that were so large and heavy they shook the floor. Their users waited anxiously for the many minutes
that frequently elapsed between reading the card deck and printing the output.

Today large sport rockets of more than 100 kilograms liftoff mass, using high power sport rocket
motors of total impulse classes up through O (40,960 newton-seconds maximum) and solid composite
propellants with a specific impulse of over 224 seconds, can legally be launched from certain sites.
High power sport rockets regularly attain altitudes over 10,000 meters, and the MIT Rocket Team (the
descendant of the MIT Model Rocket Society and its sister organization for rocket motor and
propellant research, the MIT Rocket Research Society) is planning to launch a rocket to an altitude
greater than the K\'arm\'an line (100 kilometers, the theoretical altitude limit of aircraft having
air-breathing engines and the ``edge of space'' as currently defined by the F\'ed\'eration
A\'eronautique Internationale). Maximum altitudes attained are verified by on-board recording
altimeters, transponders providing both altitude and position information, or satellite navigation
systems such as GPS. Onboard radio transmitters, instruments, and telemetry transducers use
state-of-the-art computer chips and other miniaturized integrated circuits, and rocket-mounted
photography and videography use digital technology. Boost gliders have become so aerodynamically
efficient they require dethermalizers or radio control systems to avoid being lost, and those that
have radio controls are as technically advanced as drones. Sport rockets are being equipped with
inertial guidance systems that stabilize them with active control surfaces, exhaust deflectors, and
reaction control systems, allowing large rockets to make vertical flights from easily portable launch
rails. Computers no larger than smart mobile telephones are more capable than the giant machines of
the late 1960s and early 1970s, and their users can easily instruct them from their keyboards,
provide them with input data using analog-to-digital converters, and receive the results of
calculations on high-resolution video screens or printers no larger than 1960s-vintage portable
typewriters.

As we look back over the last half century we are most gratified and greatly impressed by the
technical progress of hobby rocketry and grateful to have had the opportunity to play some part in
it.
