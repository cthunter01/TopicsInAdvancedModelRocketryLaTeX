\noindent spin in model rockets.\ \edcap{Page 251 of the 1973 edition begins in mid-sentence;
page 250 ends ``Stabilization is not, however, the only motive for inducing''. The corrected text
is in Section~\ref{ch2:sec:6.3}.} It has been found that rolling models are less subject to
``dispersion'' by horizontal winds and tipoff during launch and staging than those which are not,
where by ``dispersion'' I mean the horizontal distance of the rocket from its launch site at the
time of recovery system actuation. The roll rates used in dispersion-reduction are much lower than
those used in roll stabilization, since the rockets to which they are applied are already
statically stable; the inertial effect is generally slight and its only purpose is to induce just
enough roll coupling to distribute the effects of disturbances in a radially symmetrical fashion
about the intended direction of flight.

A roll rate may be induced in a model rocket in a variety of ways. The major aerodynamic
techniques in current use include airfoiled fins, canted fins, and ``spinnerons'' (that is, fin
tabs) as illustrated in Figure~\ref{ch2:fig:51}. Canted main propulsion or outrigger engines have
also been used to ``spin up'' model rockets, and flywheel-like devices have been installed whereby
a product $I_R\omega_z$ \emph{other} than that of the rocket airframe itself has been used to
provide inertial coupling. The number and variety of roll-inducing techniques possible is so
great, indeed, that it is impossible to write down any single analytical expression accounting
for them all. In the particular case of aerodynamically-induced roll, however, Barrowman analysis
permits computation of the equilibrium roll rate as
\begin{equation*}\tag{115}
\omega_z = \frac{6\theta V\bar{Y}_T(1 + \lambda)k_r}
  {[(1 + 3\lambda)s^{2} + 4(1 + 2\lambda)s r_t + 6(1 + \lambda)r_t^{2}]k_d}
\end{equation*}

\noindent where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0pt,label={}]
  \item $\theta$ = the angle, in radians, at which the fins are canted with respect to the
  rocket's longitudinal axis when roll is induced by canted fins
  \item $\theta = -(\alpha_0)$ when roll is induced by airfoiled fins or fins equipped with
  spinnerons
  \item $\alpha_0$ = the angle, in radians, at which each airfoiled or spinneron-equipped fin
  would have to be canted to keep the rocket's roll rate zero. For practical purposes, this angle
  is identical to what aerodynamicists call the ``angle of zero lift''. Methods of determining the
  angle of zero lift are presented in Reference~\ref{ch2:ref:12}.
  \item $V$ = the rocket's airspeed
\end{itemize}
\noindent $k_r$, the roll forcing interference coefficient, is given by
\begin{equation*}\tag{116}
\begin{split}
k_r = (1/\pi^{2})\Bigl\{&(\pi^{2}/4)[(\tau + 1)/\tau]^{2}
  + (\pi/\tau^{2})[(\tau^{2} + 1)/(\tau - 1)]^{2}\arcsin[(\tau^{2} - 1)/(\tau^{2} + 1)]\\
&- (2\pi/\tau)(\tau + 1)/(\tau - 1) + [8/(\tau - 1)^{2}]\ln[(\tau^{2} + 1)/(2\tau)]\\
&+ \{(\tau^{2} + 1)/[\tau(\tau - 1)]\}^{2}\{\arcsin[(\tau^{2} - 1)/(\tau^{2} + 1)]\}^{2}\\
&- \{4(\tau + 1)/[\tau(\tau - 1)]\}\arcsin[(\tau^{2} - 1)/(\tau^{2} + 1)]\Bigr\}
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
and $k_d$, the roll damping interference coefficient, is given by
\begin{equation*}\tag{117}
k_d = 1 + \frac{[(\tau - \lambda)/\tau] - [(1 - \lambda)/(\tau - 1)]\ln(\tau)}
  {[(\tau + 1)(\tau - \lambda)/2] - (1 - \lambda)(\tau^{3} - 1)/[3(\tau - 1)]}
\end{equation*}
$\bar{Y}_t$ is given by equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:90}, $\lambda$ is the ratio $c_t/c_r$, and $\tau$ is
the ratio $(s + r_t)/r_t$. The reader should refer back to Figure~\ref{ch2:fig:36} in
Section~\ref{ch2:sec:4.1} for additional information concerning the notation used in
equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:115} through~\eqref{ch2:eq:117}.

Given the ability to regulate the roll rate of a proposed rocket, the designer will next want to
know what roll rates are favorable to good performance. This question is generally answered by
eliminating those ranges of roll rate which are \emph{deleterious} to good performance and
stating that the ranges then remaining are acceptable. The roll rate that should be avoided at
all costs is, of course, the roll-coupled resonant rate. In models where roll is produced by the
fins this is relatively easy to do, as both the resonant frequency and the roll rate are linear
functions of airspeed. $\omega_z$ can thus be kept as nearly zero as possible (the most common
course of action), uniformly much lower than $\omega_{\mathrm{cres}}$ (the technique adopted when
dispersion-reduction is desired), or uniformly much higher than $\omega_{\mathrm{cres}}$. This last
technique is not recommended, as it results in large coupling moments, a high-drag configuration,
and (often) tangling of the shrouds of parachute recovery systems. The range of
$\beta_c = \omega_z/\omega_{nc}$ to be avoided to ensure that rolling will not amplify pitch and
yaw angles caused by disturbances more than 25\% is illustrated in Figure~\ref{ch2:fig:52}.
Perhaps you can now see the danger of resonance associated with too high a value of $I_L$: a very
high longitudinal moment of inertia means a very low roll-coupled resonant frequency, and it is
entirely possible that small fin cant angles arising from imperfections in construction will be
just sufficient to produce the low roll rate needed for resonance. Figure~\ref{ch2:fig:52} also
suggests a simple rule of thumb for preventing a rolling rocket from developing excessive pitch
and yaw angles (``coning'' about the intended direction of flight) even if it has a very low
$\zeta_c$, while avoiding the problems caused by excessive roll rate: keep $\omega_z$ less than
$0.44\omega_{nc}$, or greater than $1.35\omega_{nc}$ but not over $2.0\omega_{nc}$.
