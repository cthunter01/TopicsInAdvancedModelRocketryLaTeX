\subsection{Weight}\label{ch1:sec:2.2}

The \emph{weight} of a model rocket is the force exerted on it by the Earth's
gravitational field. A rocket of mass $m$ will have a weight given by
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:8}
\vec{w} = m\vec{g}
\end{equation}
where, as before, $\vec{g}$ is the (vector) acceleration of the Earth's gravity
field. The weight force always points in the direction of the gravity field,
which in this case is the center of the Earth---or ``straight down'' along the
local vertical. Strictly speaking, the value of $\vec{g}$ decreases with
increasing altitude above the Earth's surface according to the relation
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:9}
\frac{g}{g_0} = \left[\frac{R_0}{R}\right]^{2}
\end{equation}
where $R_0$ is the radius of the Earth, $g_0$ is the magnitude of $\vec{g}$ at
the Earth's surface, $R$ is any radius greater than $R_0$ and $g$ is the
magnitude of $\vec{g}$ at that radius. Even a 20,000-foot model rocket flight
(about 6,000 meters---much higher than any ever officially confirmed), however,
would result in an apogee value of $R$ only \emph{one tenth of one percent}
greater than $R_0$. The effect of altitude on the weight of a model rocket is,
therefore, wholly negligible. In all the calculations in this book $\vec{g}$
will be taken to have the magnitude $g_0$.

The weight of a model rocket \emph{does} vary during its flight, though. This
in-flight weight variation is due to the expulsion of mass from the rocket
motor as it produces thrust. In Section~\ref{ch1:sec:2.1} the relationship
between thrust and the rate of mass expulsion was established as
equation~\eqref{ch1:eq:2}. Equation~\eqref{ch1:eq:2} can now be used to
determine the model's mass as a function of time in the form
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:10}
m = m_0 - \int_{0}^{t} \frac{F(t)}{c}\,dt
\end{equation}
When the engine ceases burning at time $t_b$, the mass of the rocket is
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:11}
m_b = m_0 - \int_{0}^{t_b} \frac{F(t)}{c}\,dt \qquad \text{or, since}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:12}
m_f = \int_{0}^{t_b} \frac{F(t)}{c}\,dt
\end{equation}
where $m_f$ is the total mass of propellant in the engine before ignition,
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:13}
m_b = m_0 - m_f
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the weight of the rocket decreases continuously during the engine
burn and the model is lighter after burnout than at ignition. In large guided
missiles and space boosters the propellant constitutes most of the liftoff mass
of the vehicle, so that such a rocket at burnout has only a small fraction of
the mass it had at ignition. In model rockets, however, the mass change due to
the expenditure of the propellant is relatively slight. This fact, as will be
shown in Chapter~\ref{ch4}, considerably simplifies the calculation of model
rocket altitude performance.

Unlike the thrust, the direction of the weight vector remains invariant when
the rocket rotates or ``tips'' away from the vertical. The weight of a model
rocket, or for that matter any object, appears to originate from a point called
the \emph{center of mass}, which for all practical purposes within the scope of
this book may be taken as identical to the \emph{center of gravity}, or
\emph{balance point}: the point by which the object may be suspended without
showing any tendency to rotate in any direction. All rotations of a model in
flight occur about the \CG, and the weight vector always acts through the \CG{}
downward along the local vertical.

\subsection{Drag and Side Force}\label{ch1:sec:2.3}

Probably the most interesting, and certainly the most complex and difficult to
calculate, force acting on a body moving through the atmosphere is the
aerodynamic resistance to its passage, which physicists and engineers call
\emph{drag}. The drag of a body is a function of the \emph{density} and
\emph{viscosity} of the medium through which it moves, its \emph{size} and
\emph{shape}, the \emph{velocity} with which it moves, the \emph{quality of its
finish}, its \emph{detailed surface characteristics}, and its \emph{angle of
attack}.

Detailed discussions of the physical mechanisms by which all these factors
influence the drag of a model rocket will be presented in Chapter~\ref{ch3}.
For the present it will be enough to write down just a few of the most
fundamental facts about drag as it pertains to model rockets. First of all, the
total drag on a model rocket moving through air of density $\rho$ at a velocity
of magnitude $v$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:14}
D = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho \CD A_r v^{2}
\end{equation}
where $A_r$ is a \emph{reference area} which is usually taken to be the
cross-sectional area of the largest diameter body tube used in the rocket and
$\CD$ is the \emph{drag coefficient}, a dimensionless parameter which depends on
the shape of the model and its angle of attack, and also (more generally) on
the Reynolds number at which it is operating.

The \emph{angle of attack}, usually denoted by the symbol $\alpha$, is the angle
between the instantaneous velocity vector of the model's \CG{} with respect to
the air and the orientation of the rocket's longitudinal axis. The concept of
angle of attack is illustrated in Figure~\ref{ch1:fig:6}. As the angle of
attack of a model rocket increases from zero its drag coefficient also
increases, since the effective area normal---that is, perpendicular---to the
airflow which it presents becomes larger than the reference area $A_r$ used in
calculating the drag, and the effective shape of the rocket becomes more
difficult for the airflow to negotiate.

The Reynolds number, named for the English physicist Osborne Reynolds
(1842--1912), is usually denoted by $R_e$ and is a dimensionless aerodynamic
scaling factor given by the relation
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:15}
R_e = \frac{\rho}{\mu} vL
\end{equation}
where (again) $\rho$ is the density of the air, $\mu$ is the viscosity of the
air, $v$ is the magnitude of the velocity vector and $L$ is the length of the
rocket or other object of interest. There is also a \emph{kinematic viscosity},
defined as
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:16}
\nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}
\end{equation}
so that the Reynolds number is sometimes written as
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:17}
R_e = \frac{vL}{\nu}
\end{equation}
The concept of viscosity will be more fully discussed and explained in
Chapter~\ref{ch3}. For the present, the importance of
equations~\eqref{ch1:eq:15} and~\eqref{ch1:eq:17} can be summed up in two
statements:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item The flow about two geometrically similar objects is \emph{dynamically
  similar} if the Reynolds numbers for the two cases are identical; and
  \item The Reynolds number expresses the ratio of \emph{inertial} force to
  \emph{viscous} force in any given fluid-flow situation. The effects of
  \emph{viscosity} predominate at Reynolds numbers less than 1.0, while
  \emph{inertial} effects predominate at Reynolds numbers greater than 1.0.
\end{enumerate}
Statement (a) is important to the field of aerodynamic testing in which models
are used, such as wind-tunnel testing. It means, for instance, that a
quarter-scale model of an airplane placed in a wind tunnel will yield accurate
information about the full-scale prototype if the tunnel airspeed is maintained
at four times the flight speed of the prototype, so that the product $vL$ is
the same for the two cases (or if the tunnel is pressurized or some other fluid
than air is used, so that the kinematic viscosity is decreased and a lower
speed may be used). Conversely, data taken on a model rocket placed in a wind
tunnel which can only generate an airspeed of 5 meters per second may
\emph{not} apply to that same model when it is flying 150 meters per second.

Statement (b) is important when considering the effect of varying Reynolds
number on the drag coefficient. The density of sea-level air is
$1.225 \times 10^{-3}$ gram/cm.$^{3}$, while its viscosity at room temperature
($18\dg$ C) is $1.827 \times 10^{-4}$ gram/(cm.-sec.). The unit combination
gram/(cm.-sec.) is also known as the \emph{poise}. In any case, this makes the
value of $\rho/\mu$ in CGS units equal to 6.69 sec./cm.$^{2}$. With the length
of a typical model rocket being about 30 centimeters, a flight velocity of 150
meters/sec. (15,000 cm./sec.) produces a Reynolds number of about
$3 \times 10^{6}$. An airspeed of 5 meters/second, on the other hand, gives an
$R_e$ of only $1 \times 10^{5}$. Both numbers are considerably greater than one,
of course, so that inertial forces are much greater than viscous forces in all
cases involving model rockets. Still, the two cases differ by more than an
order of magnitude, so that viscous effects are \emph{thirty times} more
important in the wind-tunnel test than in flight. Consequently, the flow
behavior observed in the wind-tunnel test may be different from that actually
encountered in flight. The drag coefficient, in particular, may be somewhat
different.

Each component of a model rocket---nosecone, body tube, fins, and so
on---contributes in some measure to the overall drag on the model, and hence to
its drag coefficient. Each contributes both \emph{pressure drag} due to the
effect of its shape in displacing the airflow pattern about the rocket and
\emph{friction drag} due to the \emph{viscous shearing stress}, or
``friction'', of the air directly adjacent to its lateral surfaces. The effect
of the components in modifying the flow about each other also produces a small
amount of additional drag, called the \emph{interference drag}. Changes in
Reynolds number over a great enough range can change the value of the overall
drag coefficient through the phenomenon of \emph{transition}, which alters the
characteristics of the airflow about the rocket. At lower Reynolds numbers
(less than $5 \times 10^{5}$), where viscous forces are more important, the
flow in the \emph{boundary layer} directly adjacent to the model is likely to
be arranged in neat, smooth, microscopically-thin layers and is thus said to be
\emph{laminar}. At higher Reynolds numbers (greater than $5 \times 10^{6}$) the
importance of viscosity diminishes and the boundary layer is likely to undergo
transition and become \emph{turbulent}; the once-smooth layers mixing and
swirling down the side of the model in a rough, chaotic manner.\ednote{The 1973
text gives the laminar range as ``less than $5 \times 10^{5}$'' but the
turbulent range as ``greater than $5 \times 10^{6}$''. The two thresholds are
inconsistent (a single transition value is evidently intended; $5 \times 10^{5}$
is the usual flat-plate figure), and neither the errata sheet nor the 1994
supplement corrects them. Both are left as printed.} The
\emph{friction} drag of an object with a turbulent boundary layer is much
\emph{higher} than that of one with a laminar boundary layer, but a turbulent
boundary layer follows the contour of an object's surface better than a laminar
one and is less likely to \emph{separate} from the surface, so it may
\emph{lower} the \emph{pressure} drag. Detailed discussions of precisely how
much Reynolds number changes, with their associated transition phenomena, alter
a model rocket's drag coefficient will be presented in Chapter~\ref{ch3}. The
present brief discussion should be sufficient to give you a general idea of why
the Reynolds number is important and how it can change the drag coefficient.
Over the range of $R_e$ usually encountered in practice, values of $\CD$ for
most model rockets range from 0.35 to about 1.0, averaging 0.7 to 0.75 for
sport rockets of average design and finish quality.

Chapter~\ref{ch3} will also present a thorough treatment of methods for
calculating how a model rocket's drag coefficient increases with angle of
attack. A slightly different approach, but one which gives very nearly the same
numerical results, will be used in Chapter~\ref{ch4} for determining the effect
of dynamic oscillations (``wobbling'' of the rocket on the way up) on a model's
maximum altitude. According to this treatment, the coefficient of drag at zero
angle of attack is written $\CDo$. A constant $k$ can then be defined such that
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:18}
k \equiv \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A_r \CDo
\end{equation}
Then one can represent the drag as a function of angle of attack by
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:19}
D = \left\{k + \epsilon f(\alpha)\right\} v^{2}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is a constant and $f(\alpha)$ is some function of the angle of
attack that decreases to zero as the angle of attack itself goes to zero.
According to the method of analysis developed by James Barrowman, which is
discussed in detail in Chapter~\ref{ch2}, a rocket flying at a nonzero angle of
attack experiences a \emph{normal force} (i.e., one perpendicular to the
model's centerline) whose magnitude is given by\ednote{This paragraph, with
equations~\eqref{ch1:eq:20}, \eqref{ch1:eq:n21} and~\eqref{ch1:eq:n22}, follows
Mandell's June 1994 ``Correction to original page 40 of Chapter~1''. The 1973
text read $N = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_{n\alpha} A_r v^{2}$ for equation~(20),
continued ``where $C_{n\alpha}$ is the \emph{normal force coefficient}, whose
computation by the Barrowman method is described in Chapter~2. The component of
force $N\sin\alpha$ is an additional contribution to the \emph{drag}, while the
component $N\cos\alpha$ is a \emph{side force} \ldots'', and had no equations
corresponding to \eqref{ch1:eq:n21} and~\eqref{ch1:eq:n22}. The supplement
directs that all subsequent equation numbers be increased by 2: the 1973
equations (21) and (22) therefore now display as \eqref{ch1:eq:21}
and~\eqref{ch1:eq:22}.}
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:20}
N = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_N A_r v^{2}
\end{equation}
where $C_N$ is the \emph{normal force coefficient} whose computation by the
Barrowman method is described in Chapter~\ref{ch2}. For angles of attack that
are not too large $C_N$ is very nearly directly proportional to $\alpha$. The
variation of $C_N$ with $\alpha$ can then be expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:n21}
C_N = \CNa \cdot \alpha
\end{equation}
where $\CNa$ is the slope of the curve of the normal force coefficient versus
angle of attack, often referred to in an abbreviated fashion as the
\emph{normal force curve slope}. Then
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:n22}
N = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho \CNa A_r v^{2}\alpha
\end{equation}
The component of force $N\sin\alpha$ is an additional contribution to the
\emph{drag}, while the component of force $N\cos\alpha$ is a \emph{side force}
tending to deflect the rocket's path by causing an acceleration component
normal to the current direction of flight. The resolution of the normal force
is shown in Figure~\ref{ch1:fig:7}.

Now at small angles of attack (less than about $15\dg$) $\sin\alpha$ is very
nearly equal to the value of $\alpha$ itself in \emph{radians} (one radian
equals $57.3\dg$), while $\cos\alpha$ is very nearly equal to 1.0. Accordingly,
one can make the \emph{small-angle approximations}
\begin{align}
N\sin\alpha &\cong N\alpha \label{ch1:eq:21}\\
S &\equiv N\cos\alpha \label{ch1:eq:22}\\
  &\cong N \notag
\end{align}
where $S$ denotes the magnitude of the side force. Now by inspection of
equations~\eqref{ch1:eq:19}, \eqref{ch1:eq:n22}, and~\eqref{ch1:eq:21}, one
might be tempted to deduce that\ednote{The 1973 text cited ``equations (19),
(20), and (21)'' and wrote $\epsilon = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_{n\alpha} A_r$. After
the June 1994 replacement of page 40 the 1973 equation (21) displays as
\eqref{ch1:eq:21}, and the relation that, with \eqref{ch1:eq:19}
and~\eqref{ch1:eq:21}, yields the deduction below is the new
equation~\eqref{ch1:eq:n22}, $N = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho \CNa A_r v^{2}\alpha$,
rather than the rewritten \eqref{ch1:eq:20}; the citation has been changed
accordingly. The 1973 symbol $C_{n\alpha}$, which the 1973 text called the
normal force coefficient, is here the quantity the 1994 text names the normal
force curve slope, and has been written $\CNa$ to match.}
\begin{equation*}
\epsilon = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho \CNa A_r \qquad \text{and}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
f(\alpha) = \alpha^{2}
\end{equation*}
Unfortunately, things are not quite that simple. The drag increase due to the
normal force is only one of several contributions to the increase in drag
produced by a nonzero angle of attack. As explained in Chapter~\ref{ch3},
viscous cross-flow and increased interference effects cause the increase in
drag due to angle of attack to be somewhat greater than one would predict from
Barrowman analysis alone. It will, however, be found quite accurate to
represent $f(\alpha)$ by $\alpha^{2}$, and this property will be used in
Chapter~\ref{ch4} to estimate the effects of the oscillatory responses to
various in-flight perturbations on altitude capability.

In Figure~\ref{ch1:fig:8} a complete vector diagram of all the forces
influencing the center-of-mass motion of a model rocket in flight is presented.
The making of such a drawing, called a \emph{free-body diagram}, is one of the
fundamental steps in the solution of any problem in statics or dynamics since
it enables one to readily visualize all the components of the forces required
to write down the equations of motion. You may find it helpful to consult
Figure~\ref{ch1:fig:8} of this chapter again when reading Chapter~\ref{ch4}.
