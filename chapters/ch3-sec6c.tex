\subsection{General Analysis of the Datcom Method}\label{ch3:sec:6.3}

\subsubsection{The General Configuration Rocket (GCR)}\label{ch3:sec:6.3.1}

The preceding section was intended to familiarize the reader with the application of the
\emph{Datcom} method to a particular problem. In this section the drag coefficient equations will
be cast into special forms, particularly applicable to model rockets, which will clarify the
relationships between rocket geometry and drag. This general, nondimensional approach will be
utilized to discover the behavior of the drag coefficient and the drag force with respect to
changing Reynolds number in Sections~\ref{ch3:sec:6.3.2} and~\ref{ch3:sec:6.3.3}.

We begin by listing in general form the \emph{functional dependence} of the drag coefficients upon
the variables of the \emph{Datcom} equations (equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:158}
through~\eqref{ch3:eq:162}):
\begin{align}
(C_{Df})_b &= G_1\left(B, R_\ell, \frac{\ell_b}{d_m}, \frac{S_s}{S_m}\right) \label{ch3:eq:175}\\
C_{Db} &= G_2\left(B, R_\ell, \frac{\ell_b}{d_m}, \frac{S_s}{S_m}, \frac{d_b}{d_m}\right)
  \label{ch3:eq:176}\\
(\CDo)_B &= G_3\left(B, R_\ell, \frac{\ell_b}{d_m}, \frac{S_s}{S_m}, \frac{d_b}{d_m}\right)
  \label{ch3:eq:177}\\
(\CDo)_F &= G_4\left(B, R_c, \frac{t}{c}, \frac{S_F}{S_m}\right) \label{ch3:eq:178}\\
(\CDo)_{FB} &= G_5\left(B, R_\ell, \frac{\ell_b}{d_m}, \frac{S_s}{S_m}, \frac{d_b}{d_m}, R_c,
  \frac{t}{c}, \frac{S_F}{S_m}\right) \label{ch3:eq:179}
\end{align}
where the right-hand sides of the equations are read as ``$G_1$ of $B$, $R_\ell$,
$\frac{\ell_b}{d_m}$, $\frac{S_s}{S_m}$'', and so on. They are simply a form of mathematical
shorthand to indicate that the drag coefficients depend on the quantities in parentheses on the
right in some (as yet unspecified) fashion.

Theoretically, we could obtain a graphical relationship between any one of the above drag
coefficients and one of its variables by assigning constant values to all the other variables in
the function. As a practical matter, however, this would not be very illuminating, as variables
like $S_s/S_m$ and $S_F/S_m$ do not convey any concept of shape and are difficult to visualize.
They can be converted to expressions involving only linear dimensions, though, if we assume
specific shapes for the nosecone, fins, main body and boattail (if any) of the rocket.

For this reason, we adopt as the basis for the remainder of the analysis in this section the
configuration shown in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:50}: a rocket consisting of an ogive nosecone, a
circular cylindrical body, rectangular fins, and a conical boattail. This class of vehicles will
henceforth be referred to as the General Configuration Rocket (GCR), since by varying its
proportions one can obtain reasonable approximations to the shape of many single-staged model
rockets. It should be noted that the fins enter the \emph{Datcom} equations only through their
surface area $S_F$ and the thickness ratio $t/c$; hence no generality is lost by assuming
rectangular fins for the GCR. The shape of the fins is important only in determining the drag and
side force due to a \emph{nonzero angle of attack}, whereas the \emph{Datcom} equations presented
here involve only the determination of the zero-lift drag coefficient. Furthermore, the GCR has no
specific size until a numerical value for one of its linear dimensions is specified; the
variations depicted in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:50} could be ten centimeters or one meter in length.
This ``nondimensionality'' is the most valuable property of the GCR concept.

    possible variations.}

We can now write for the GCR,
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:180}
\left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{GCR}} = \left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{OGIVE}}
  + \left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{CYL}} + \left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{BOATTAIL}}
\end{equation}
The approximate expression for the ogive nose is
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:181}
\left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{OGIVE}} \cong 2.7\,\frac{\ell_N}{d_m}
  \qquad \left(\frac{\ell_N}{d_m} > 1.5\right)
\end{equation}
and the exact equation for the cylindrical body is
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:182}
\left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{CYL}} = 4\,\frac{\ell_s}{d_m}
\end{equation}
For the conical boattail,\ednote{Both equations are printed with a minus sign in the leading
factor: $(d_m - d_b)$ before the radical in~\eqref{ch3:eq:183a} and $(1 - d_b/d_m)$
in~\eqref{ch3:eq:183b}, as are equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:172a} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:172b}. The factors
$(d_m + d_b)$ and $(1 + d_b/d_m)$ are evidently meant: the lateral area of a conical frustum is
$\pi(d_m + d_b)/2$ times its slant height $\ell_T[1 + ((d_m - d_b)/2\ell_T)^2]^{1/2}$, so the
$(d_m - d_b)$ under the radical is correct, and with the printed sign in the leading factor a
boattail with $d_b = d_m$, which is a cylinder, would have no surface instead of the $4\ell_T/d_m$ that equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:182} gives for a cylinder of that length. The printed
factor is carried into equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:184} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:199}; for the GCR-x it makes
the boattail's $S_s/S_m$ 1.2 instead of 10.8. Neither the errata nor the supplements correct this;
the equations are kept as printed.}
\begin{subequations}\label{ch3:eq:183}
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:183a}
\left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{BOATTAIL}} = \frac{2(d_m - d_b)\ell_T}{d_m^2}
  \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{d_m - d_b}{2\ell_T}\right)^2}
\end{equation}
which, for any practical boattail, can be accurately approximated as
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:183b}
\left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{BOATTAIL}} \cong 2\left(1 - \frac{d_b}{d_m}\right)
  \frac{\ell_T}{d_m}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
Hence,
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:184}
\left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{GCR}} = 2.7\,\frac{\ell_N}{d_m} + 4\,\frac{\ell_s}{d_m}
  + 2\left(1 - \frac{d_b}{d_m}\right)\frac{\ell_T}{d_m}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:175}, \eqref{ch3:eq:176}, and~\eqref{ch3:eq:177} can also be written
\begin{align}
(C_{Df})_b &= H_1\left(B, R_\ell, \frac{d_b}{d_m}, \left\{\frac{\ell_b}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_N}{d_m},
  \frac{\ell_s}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_T}{d_m}\right\}\right) \label{ch3:eq:185}\\
C_{Db} &= H_2\left(B, R_\ell, \frac{d_b}{d_m}, \left\{\frac{\ell_b}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_N}{d_m},
  \frac{\ell_s}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_T}{d_m}\right\}\right) \label{ch3:eq:186}\\
(\CDo)_B &= H_3\left(B, R_\ell, \frac{d_b}{d_m}, \left\{\frac{\ell_b}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_N}{d_m},
  \frac{\ell_s}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_T}{d_m}\right\}\right) \label{ch3:eq:187}
\end{align}
where the letters $H$ again just refer to the fact that the value of the quantity on the left-hand
side of each equation depends upon the values of the quantities in parentheses on the right-hand
side. The bracketed expression $\left\{\frac{\ell_b}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_N}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_s}{d_m},
\frac{\ell_T}{d_m}\right\}$ means that only three of the four length-to-diameter ratios may be
chosen to specify the problem, since choosing values for any three determines the value of the
fourth. This follows from the fact that
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:188}
\frac{\ell_b}{d_m} = \frac{\ell_N}{d_m} + \frac{\ell_s}{d_m} + \frac{\ell_T}{d_m}
\end{equation}

In a similar manner, $S_F/S_m$ may be transformed:
\begin{align}
S_F &= 4\sigma_F = 4\left(c\,\frac{b}{2}\right) = 2cb \label{ch3:eq:189}\\
\frac{S_F}{S_m} &= \frac{2cb}{\pi\dfrac{d_m^2}{4}}
  = \left(\frac{8}{\pi}\right)\left(\frac{c}{d_m}\right)\left(\frac{b}{d_m}\right)
  \label{ch3:eq:190}
\end{align}
Furthermore,
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:191}
R_\ell = \frac{U_\infty\ell_b}{\nu} \qquad\text{and}\qquad R_c = \frac{U_\infty c}{\nu}
\end{equation}
and $R_c$ can therefore be eliminated by substituting
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:192}
R_c = \left(\frac{c}{\ell_b}\right)R_\ell = \frac{(c/d_m)}{(\ell_b/d_m)}\,R_\ell
\end{equation}
Consequently, equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:178} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:193}
(\CDo)_F = H_4\left(B, R_\ell, \frac{\ell_b}{d_m}, \frac{t}{c}, \frac{c}{d_m}, \frac{b}{d_m}\right)
\end{equation}
The fin-body interference drag coefficient, which is one component of the fin drag coefficient, is
given by
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:194}
C_{DI} = 2(C_f)_F\left(1 + 2\,\frac{t}{c}\right)\frac{S_F - S_E}{S_m}
\end{equation}
For the GCR, it is found that
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:195}
\frac{S_F - S_E}{S_m} = \frac{4}{\pi}\left[\left(\frac{c}{d_m}\right)\left(\frac{d_b}{d_m}\right)
  + \left(\frac{c}{d_m}\right)^2\left(\frac{d_m}{\ell_T}\right)\left(1 - \frac{d_b}{d_m}\right)\right]
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:196}
C_{DI} = H_5\left(B, R_\ell, \frac{\ell_b}{d_m}, \frac{t}{c}, \frac{c}{d_m}, \frac{b}{d_m},
  \frac{d_b}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_T}{d_m}\right)
\end{equation}
The total drag coefficient is then seen to exhibit the functional dependence
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:197}
(\CDo)_{FB} = H_6\left(B, R_\ell, \frac{d_b}{d_m}, \frac{t}{c}, \frac{c}{d_m}, \frac{b}{d_m},
  \left\{\frac{\ell_b}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_N}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_s}{d_m}, \frac{\ell_T}{d_m}\right\}\right)
\end{equation}

Nine independent, dimensionless variables completely determine the overall drag coefficient and
its various constituents, as seen in equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:185}, \eqref{ch3:eq:186},
\eqref{ch3:eq:187}, \eqref{ch3:eq:193}, \eqref{ch3:eq:196}, and~\eqref{ch3:eq:197}. Seven of these
are strictly geometrical factors; the remaining two ($B$ and $R_\ell$) are related to the
interaction of the rocket and the fluid through which it moves. This set of variables is not
unique; for example, since
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:198}
\left(\frac{t}{c}\right)\left(\frac{c}{d_m}\right) = \frac{t}{d_m}
\end{equation}
any two of these three quantities may be used to replace $t/c$ and $c/d_m$. Although each drag
coefficient is a function of more variables than before (equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:175}
through~\eqref{ch3:eq:179}), the problem has been simplified because there is a direct and visible
relationship between $(\CDo)_{FB}$ and the pertinent factors of rocket geometry and vehicle-fluid
interaction. The \emph{Datcom} equations, expressed in terms of these 9 variables for the GCR, are
as follows:
\begin{align}
(C_{Df})_b &= \left[1 + \frac{60}{(\ell_b/d_m)^3} + .0025\,\frac{\ell_b}{d_m}\right]
  \left[2.7\,\frac{\ell_N}{d_m} + 4\,\frac{\ell_s}{d_m}
  + 2\left(1 - \frac{d_b}{d_m}\right)\frac{\ell_T}{d_m}\right](C_f)_B \label{ch3:eq:199}\\
C_{Db} &= .029\left(\frac{d_b}{d_m}\right)^3\Big/\sqrt{(C_{Df})_b} \label{ch3:eq:200}\\
(\CDo)_F &= \frac{16}{\pi}(C_f)_F\left(\frac{c}{d_m}\right)\left(\frac{b}{d_m}\right)
  \left(1 + 2\,\frac{t}{c}\right) \label{ch3:eq:201}\\
C_{DI} &= \frac{8}{\pi}(C_f)_F\left(1 + 2\,\frac{t}{c}\right)
  \left[\left(\frac{c}{d_m}\right)\left(\frac{d_b}{d_m}\right)
  + \left(\frac{c}{d_m}\right)^2\left(\frac{d_m}{\ell_T}\right)\left(1 - \frac{d_b}{d_m}\right)\right]
  \label{ch3:eq:202}
\end{align}
where\ednote{The side conditions of equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:204a} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:204b} are
printed $(R_\ell < R_{\mathrm{crit}})$ and $(R_\ell \ge R_{\mathrm{crit}})$, as for the body.
Transition on the fins depends on the chord Reynolds number $R_c = (c/\ell_b)R_\ell$ of
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:192}, so the conditions evidently meant are
$R_\ell < (\ell_b/c)R_{\mathrm{crit}}$ and $R_\ell \ge (\ell_b/c)R_{\mathrm{crit}}$: these give the
$5.14 \times 10^{6}$ $(= (18/1.75) \times 5 \times 10^{5})$ used for the fins of the GCR-x below.
Neither the errata nor the supplements correct this; the conditions are kept as printed.}
% (203a)-(204b) are one block in the book, all four '=' in one column and the side conditions in
% another; two subequations groups cannot share one alignment, so the letters are \tag'ged in one
% align*. The two \refstepcounter lines give the group labels (203), (204) (cited below as
% "(199) through (204)") and advance the counter so that the next equation is (205).
\refstepcounter{equation}\label{ch3:eq:203}%
\refstepcounter{equation}\label{ch3:eq:204}%
\begin{align*}
(C_f)_B &= \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{R_\ell}} && (R_\ell < R_{\mathrm{crit}})
  \tag{203a}\label{ch3:eq:203a}\\
(C_f)_B &= \frac{0.074}{(R_\ell)^{1/5}} - \frac{B}{R_\ell} && (R_\ell \ge R_{\mathrm{crit}})
  \tag{203b}\label{ch3:eq:203b}\\
(C_f)_F &= \sqrt{\frac{\ell_b}{c}}\,\frac{1.328}{\sqrt{R_\ell}} && (R_\ell < R_{\mathrm{crit}})
  \tag{204a}\label{ch3:eq:204a}\\
(C_f)_F &= \left(\frac{\ell_b}{c}\right)^{1/5}\frac{0.074}{(R_\ell)^{1/5}}
  - \left(\frac{\ell_b}{c}\right)\frac{B}{R_\ell} && (R_\ell \ge R_{\mathrm{crit}})
  \tag{204b}\label{ch3:eq:204b}
\end{align*}
and the overall zero-lift drag coefficient is given by
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:205}
(\CDo)_{FB} = (C_{Df})_b + C_{Db} + (\CDo)_F
\end{equation}

It is now a relatively straightforward matter to produce graphs depicting the behavior of any of
the above drag coefficients as one of its parameters is varied. Some of these relationships will
be more useful than others; it does not seem productive, for example, to determine the effect of
the nose fineness ratio on the base drag coefficient. To illustrate such an analysis, we instead
consider the far more important relationship between drag coefficient and Reynolds number---which,
for a rocket of given length, translates directly into a relationship between drag coefficient and
airspeed.

\subsubsection{Dependence of the Drag Coefficient on Reynolds Number for the General Configuration
Rocket}\label{ch3:sec:6.3.2}

The Reynolds number $R_\ell$ is itself a function of three variables, $U_\infty$, $\ell_b$, and
$\nu$. If the relationship between drag coefficient and $R_\ell$ is known, therefore, this
relationship can be used to determine $\CD$ as a function of velocity by assuming numerical values
for the 8 other quantities in the \emph{Datcom} equations:
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:206}
\CD = f(U_\infty) \qquad \left(\frac{\ell_b}{\nu} = \text{constant}\right)
\end{equation}
A knowledge of this function is essential for accurate performance calculations. In particular,
an assurance that $\CD$ does not vary greatly over the expected velocity range encountered by a
given model in flight is required before a constant $\CD$ can be used in the performance
calculations for that model with a high degree of confidence. To determine the variation of $\CD$
with $R_\ell$ for a particular example of the GCR (denoted GCR-x) the following values were
assigned to the 8 variables:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{array}{l@{\qquad}l@{\qquad}l@{\qquad}l}
\dfrac{d_b}{d_m} = .8 & \dfrac{\ell_T}{d_m} = 3 & \dfrac{\ell_N}{d_m} = 3.5
  & \dfrac{\ell_s}{d_m} = 11.5\\[2.5ex]
\dfrac{t}{c} = .02 & \dfrac{c}{d_m} = 1.75 & \dfrac{b}{d_m} = 5
  & B = 1735 \quad (R_{\mathrm{crit}} = 5 \times 10^{5})
\end{array}
\end{equation*}
The overall fineness ratio $\ell_b/d_m$ is thus 18, making the GCR-x a fairly slender model.
Substituting these values into equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:199} through~\eqref{ch3:eq:204}, we
obtain\ednote{With these values equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:199} gives
$(C_{Df})_b = 1.055 \times 56.65\,(C_f)_B = 59.8(C_f)_B$ with the equation as printed, or
$1.055 \times 66.25\,(C_f)_B = 69.9(C_f)_B$ with the frustum factor $(1 + d_b/d_m)$ (see the note on
the boattail factor of equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:183a} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:183b}). The printed
coefficient 82.8 would need $S_s/S_m = 78.5$, which these values do not give. Table~\ref{ch3:tab:6}
and Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:51}, and the drag forces of Table~\ref{ch3:tab:7} and
Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:52}, are computed with 82.8. Neither the errata nor the supplements correct
this; the coefficient is kept as printed.}
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
&\left.\begin{aligned}
(C_{Df})_b &= 82.8(C_f)_B\\
C_{Db} &= \frac{.0149}{\sqrt{(C_{Df})_b}}
\end{aligned}\ \right\}\ (C_{Df})_b + C_{Db} = (\CDo)_B\\
&\begin{aligned}
C_{DI} &= 4.25(C_f)_F\\
(\CDo)_F &= 46.4(C_f)_F
\end{aligned}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
where
\begin{align*}
(C_f)_B &= \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{R_\ell}}
  && \left(R_\ell = \frac{U_\infty\ell_b}{\nu} < 5 \times 10^{5}\right)\\
(C_f)_B &= \frac{0.074}{(R_\ell)^{1/5}} - \frac{1735}{R_\ell} && (R_\ell \ge 5 \times 10^{5})\\
(C_f)_F &= \frac{4.25}{\sqrt{R_\ell}} && (R_\ell < 5.14 \times 10^{6})\\
(C_f)_F &= \frac{0.118}{(R_\ell)^{1/5}} - \frac{17{,}900}{R_\ell} && (R_\ell \ge 5.14 \times 10^{6})
\end{align*}
and the overall zero-lift drag coefficient is therefore
\begin{equation*}
(\CDo)_{FB} = 82.8(C_f)_B + \frac{.0149}{\sqrt{82.8(C_f)_B}} + 46.4(C_f)_F
\end{equation*}

The results obtained for Reynolds numbers between $1 \times 10^{4}$ and $1 \times 10^{7}$ are
summarized in Table~\ref{ch3:tab:6} and presented graphically in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:51}. The
total drag coefficient $(\CDo)_{FB}$ has a very interesting shape which exhibits three main
phases.

At Reynolds numbers less than $5 \times 10^{5}$ the flow over the entire rocket is laminar and
Phase I behavior of the total drag coefficient is observed. At small Reynolds numbers,
$(\CDo)_{FB}$ is extremely large, achieving a value of 3.08 for $R_\ell = 1 \times 10^{4}$. This
behavior is expected, as the boundary layer thickens with decreasing Reynolds number and viscous
forces play a much greater role in the flow behavior than is the case at higher Reynolds numbers.
For a model rocket of 30 centimeters length, a Reynolds number of $10^{4}$ corresponds to an
airspeed of only about 0.6 meter/second. A well-designed model rocket does not leave its launcher
with a velocity much less than 9 meters/second, so the behavior of the drag coefficient at such
low Reynolds numbers is not of much interest in model rocket flight calculations.

At Reynolds numbers between $5 \times 10^{5}$ and $5 \times 10^{6}$ the rocket is operating in
Phase 2 flight. When the critical Reynolds number $R_\ell = 5 \times 10^{5}$ is attained, a region
of turbulence begins to grow from the model's base forward. This is reflected in an
\emph{increase} in the body drag coefficient $(\CDo)_B$ with increasing Reynolds number.
Simultaneously, however, there is pure laminar flow over the fins, and from
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:204a} it is seen that $(\CDo)_F$ will \emph{decrease} with increasing
Reynolds number. The net effect of these two opposing trends is to hold $(\CDo)_{FB}$ virtually
constant in this phase, denoted the \emph{body transition zone} on the graph of
Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:51}. $(\CDo)_B$ does not increase indefinitely, however, as it too is subject
to opposing phenomena. From $R_\ell = 5 \times 10^{5}$ to $R_\ell = 2 \times 10^{6}$, the increase
in skin-friction drag due to the growing turbulent region on the body is greater than the decrease
experienced in both the laminar and turbulent regions due to the inverse variation of $(C_f)_B$
with $R_\ell$ (see equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:203a} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:203b}); thus $(\CDo)_B$
increases. At $R_\ell = 2.5 \times 10^{6}$, however, the body is mostly turbulent and the
important effect becomes the reduction in $(C_f)_B$ with increasing Reynolds number. Hence, in the
upper range of the body transition zone, both $(\CDo)_B$ and $(\CDo)_F$ are decreasing, and
$(\CDo)_{FB}$ decreases with them. Phase 2 is the flight regime of greatest interest to the model
rocketeer, as most model rocket flight occurs in this range of Reynolds numbers. A
30-centimeter-long rocket is in Phase 2 flight whenever its velocity is between about 30 and 300
meters/second.

At a Reynolds number of $5.14 \times 10^{6}$, the fins of the GCR-x experience the development of
a turbulent boundary layer beginning near the trailing edge and the rocket enters Phase 3 flight.
In this phase the behavior of $(\CDo)_F$ is completely analogous to that of $(\CDo)_B$ in Phase 2.
If Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:51} were extended to higher Reynolds numbers, $(\CDo)_F$ would be seen to
attain a peak and then decrease again. In essence, the roles of $(\CDo)_B$ and $(\CDo)_F$ are
reversed in Phases 2 and 3 with respect to their effects on $(\CDo)_{FB}$. Phase 3, labeled the
\emph{fin transition zone} in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:51}, is rarely attained in model rocket flight,
due to limitations on engine power and the rapidly increasing magnitude of the drag \emph{force}
at the velocities required to produce these high Reynolds numbers. Of course, rockets with very
large fin chords, comparable to the rocket length $\ell_b$, will attain phase 3 at comparatively
low Reynolds numbers---but this is an uncommon occurrence. The effects of compressibility
generally become noticeable in Phase 3, or even in the upper range of Phase 2, so our analysis is
not completely valid for these high-velocity regimes.

The base drag coefficient is essentially constant over the entire spectrum of Reynolds numbers,
attaining its maximum value when $(\CDo)_B$ attains its minimum value---which occurs at
$R_\ell = 5 \times 10^{5}$. It is a relatively insignificant contribution to $(\CDo)_{FB}$ at low
Reynolds numbers, but in Phase 2 or Phase 3 flight it represents about 7\% of the total drag
coefficient.

Although the curves of Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:51} were determined from specific values for the
\emph{Datcom} variables, their general behavior will not be altered by changes in the values of
these variables. For example, an increase in $\frac{\ell_N}{d_m}$, $\frac{\ell_s}{d_m}$, or
$\frac{\ell_T}{d_m}$, or any combination of these three, while the original values of all the
other variables are maintained, will widen the body transition zone, as transition on the fins
will now occur at a higher value of $R_\ell$ (although $R_{\mathrm{crit}}$ remains unchanged).
Furthermore, $(\CDo)_B$ and $(\CDo)_{FB}$ will be shifted slightly upwards, while $C_{Db}$ will be
decreased everywhere. Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:51} thus presents the correct \emph{shape} of the drag
coefficient vs.\ Reynolds number curves for any model rocket which can be represented (even
approximately) by some variation of the GCR. This category includes a great many slender
($\frac{\ell_b}{d_m} \ge 5$) single-staged model rockets.

To determine the drag coefficient as a function of velocity $U_\infty$, it is required to know
only the values of $\nu$ and $\ell_b$. While $\nu$ does vary somewhat with altitude as discussed
in Section~\ref{ch3:sec:2.1}, one may reasonably assume a constant value of
$\nu = 1.495 \times 10^{-5}$ meter$^{2}$/second, giving
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:207}
U_\infty = \frac{1.495 \times 10^{-5}}{\ell_b}\,R_\ell
\end{equation}
Multiplying the abscissa $R_\ell$ of Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:51} by
$\frac{1.495 \times 10^{-5}}{\ell_b}$ converts the Reynolds number axis to a velocity axis,
measured in meters/second. For a rocket of 30 centimeters length,
\begin{equation*}
U_\infty = 4.975 \times 10^{-5}\,R_\ell
\end{equation*}
The body transition zone will then begin at an airspeed of 24.9 meters/second, and if the rocket
has the same geometric proportions as the GCR-x, fin transition begins at 256 meters/second.

In general, obtaining a plot of drag coefficient versus Reynolds number, as in
Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:51}, requires a considerable amount of calculation. This work cannot be
avoided if maximum accuracy is desired, as for theoretical predictions of altitude. As the
following section will show, however, it \emph{is} possible to choose a single value of the drag
coefficient $(\CDo)_{FB}$ which, if used in performance calculations, yields a good approximation
to the ``exact'' behavior predicted by the \emph{Datcom} method.

\subsubsection{Dependence of the Drag Force on Reynolds Number for the General Configuration
Rocket}\label{ch3:sec:6.3.3}

The drag force $D$ is expressed in terms of the drag coefficient as
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:208}
D = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2\CD S_m
\end{equation}
To eliminate $S_m$ and $U_\infty$, we write
\begin{equation*}
S_m = \frac{\pi d_m^2}{4}
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*}
U_\infty^2 = \frac{R_\ell^2\nu^2}{\ell_b^2}
\end{equation*}
Then
\begin{equation*}
D = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\left(\frac{R_\ell^2\nu^2}{\ell_b^2}\right)\CD\left(\frac{\pi d_m^2}{4}\right)
\end{equation*}
and finally
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:209}
D = \left[\frac{(\pi/8)\rho\nu^2}{(\ell_b/d_m)^2}\right]\CD R_\ell^2
\end{equation}

Since $\CD$ is known as a function of $R_\ell$ from a plot like that of Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:51},
compiled from \emph{Datcom} calculations, it is possible to determine the behavior of the drag
force as a function of $R_\ell$ using equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:209}. Assuming
$\nu = 1.495 \times 10^{-5}$ meter$^{2}$/second and $\rho = 1.225$ kilograms/meter$^{3}$
(sea-level atmosphere), and $\frac{\ell_b}{d_m} = 18$ as for the GCR-x, we have
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:210}
D = 3.33 \times 10^{-13}\,\CD R_\ell^2
\end{equation}
for the drag force in newtons.

The column headed $D_e$ in Table~\ref{ch3:tab:7} summarizes the results obtained from
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:210} over the Reynolds number range $1 \times 10^{4}$ through
$1 \times 10^{7}$. For Reynolds numbers up to about $1 \times 10^{5}$, the drag force is very small
compared with either the rocket weight (very few model rockets weigh less than 0.25 newton at
liftoff) or the thrust levels commonly encountered in model rocket engines. The drag force is so
minute in this flight regime that in the graphical representation of the data shown in
Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:52}, no distinction between values of the drag force predicted by
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:210} and by the assumption of a constant $\CD$ can be seen.

The reason for this behavior is that, although $(\CDo)_{FB}$ is very large for values of $R_\ell$
less than $10^{5}$, the quadratic dependence of the drag force on the Reynolds number drives $D$
downwards as $R_\ell$ decreases. Since the laminar skin-friction coefficient is inversely
proportional to the \emph{square root} of $R_\ell$, $(\CDo)_{FB}$ cannot increase rapidly enough to
offset this effect.

As the Reynolds number increases into the body transition zone, the drag force climbs rapidly,
experiencing a tenfold increase between $R_\ell = 6 \times 10^{5}$ and $R_\ell = 2 \times 10^{6}$.
Plotted along with the ``exact'' function $D_e$ in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:52} is an
\emph{approximating function} for the drag $D_a$, derived from equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:210} by
assuming the drag coefficient to be \emph{constant} over the entire Reynolds number range. The
value I have elected to choose for this constant is the value of $(\CDo)_{FB}$ at the initiation of
Phase 2 flight, or $R_\ell = 5 \times 10^{5}$. From Table~\ref{ch3:tab:6} we read this as
$(\CDo)_{FB} = 0.473$, so
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:211}
D = 1.58 \times 10^{-13}\,R_\ell^2
\end{equation}
gives the approximate drag force in newtons.

From inspection of Table~\ref{ch3:tab:7}, it may be seen that this function approximates $D_e$
quite closely over the range of Reynolds numbers of interest in model rocket flight, deviating less
than 10\% from the exact function for Reynolds numbers between $4 \times 10^{5}$ and
$2.2 \times 10^{6}$. Furthermore, although the percentage error is large below
$R_\ell = 4 \times 10^{5}$ (as $\CD$ attains large values in the exact calculation), the absolute
magnitude of the drag force is so small that these deviations are insignificant in the calculation
of model rocket performance. It is not necessary to know whether the drag force is 1/1000 of a
newton or 2/1000 of a newton when the thrust and weight of the vehicle are both three or four
orders of magnitude greater than these values.

At higher Reynolds numbers---that is, $R_\ell$ greater than $5 \times 10^{5}$---$D_a$ represents a
good approximation to the actual drag force because, as we saw in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:51}, the drag
coefficient is very nearly constant in the body transition zone. The value of $(\CDo)_{FB}$ at
$R_\ell = 5 \times 10^{5}$ was chosen as the approximate, constant $\CD$ for the calculations on
which Table~\ref{ch3:tab:7} and Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:52} are based for two major reasons: first,
because $R_\ell = 5 \times 10^{5}$ may be considered to represent the onset of body transition for
all model rockets, regardless of configuration; and second, because the exact magnitude of the drag
is obtainable from this value of $\CD$ to within 10\% up to Reynolds numbers in the neighborhood of
$2.2 \times 10^{6}$, very nearly the practical limit of $R_\ell$ encountered during the flight of
small- to moderate-sized model rockets.

This result can be stated in the form of a semiempirical rule: for single-staged model rockets not
expected to exceed a Reynolds number of about $3 \times 10^{6}$ in flight, the assumption of a
constant $\CD$ with a value equal to that attained at $R_\ell = 5 \times 10^{5}$ yields acceptable
estimates of performance when used in closed-form altitude calculations requiring the assumption
of a constant $\CD$.

converted to a velocity axis by applying the coordinate transformation given in
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:207}.
