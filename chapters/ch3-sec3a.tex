\section{Viscous (Skin-Friction) Drag}\label{ch3:sec:3}

\subsection{The Importance of Viscosity in Real Fluid Flow}\label{ch3:sec:3.1}

In Section~\ref{ch3:sec:2} the skin-friction drag was defined as
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:33}
D_v = \iint_{S} \tau_o \cos(\vec{t},\vec{V})\,dS
\end{equation}
where $\tau_o$ is the skin-friction force per unit area at the surface of the
model (equal to the viscous shearing stress in the air directly adjacent to
the model surface). Recalling equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:12b}, we can write
$\tau_o$ as
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:34}
\tau_o = \mu\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)_{y=0}
\end{equation}
where we have adopted a ``local'' coordinate system in which the $y$-axis
extends perpendicularly upwards from the point on the rocket's surface at
which $\tau_o$ is being evaluated. Earlier we saw, via an estimate of typical
model rocket Reynolds numbers, that inertial forces are about a million times
greater than viscous forces at average model rocket flight speeds. Why, then,
not ignore the viscous forces completely in our analysis?

The mathematical model which corresponds to this assumption is the so-called
``perfect fluid'' of classical hydrodynamics. The theory of the perfect fluid
model, which assumes a fluid that is incompressible and has a coefficient of
viscosity of zero, was well developed before the beginning of the 20th Century
since the mathematical simplifications permitted by the perfect fluid
assumption are considerable. The results one obtains from calculations based
on classical hydrodynamics, however, assert that the drag on a closed body of
\emph{any shape} moving at constant velocity through a perfect fluid is
exactly \emph{zero}. This prediction was so contradictory to all experimental
evidence---even that available in the mid-19th Century---that it came to be
called d'Alembert's Paradox. It was apparent that one of the fundamental
assumptions of the ideal fluid theory was physically inaccurate.

In 1904 the great German scientist Ludwig Prandtl (1875--1953) resolved this
dilemma by introducing the concept of the \emph{boundary layer}. According to
this theory, the influence of viscosity in fluids whose viscosity is small (or
in situations where the Reynolds number is large) is confined to a thin region
near the surface of any solid body immersed in the flow. The fluid obeys the
rule of \emph{no slip} at the wall---contrary to the behavior of the perfect
fluid, which was allowed to flow past a body freely---and hence the fluid
velocity must increase from zero at the body wall to the full free-stream
value in a very short distance, perhaps a few hundredths or thousandths of a
centimeter (note that the terms ``wall'' and ``surface'' are being used
interchangeably in this discussion). This condition requires that the fluid
velocity increase very rapidly with distance from the body surface; i.e., that
the \emph{velocity gradient} $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$ be very large in
the boundary layer, and hence $\tau_o$ in equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:34} may
\emph{not} be negligible even if $\mu$ is small.

This theory essentially divides the flow about a body at high Reynolds numbers
into two regions. Within the thin boundary layer viscous forces are of about
the same magnitude as inertial forces, and hence cannot be ignored. Outside
the boundary layer, the flow conforms very closely to the behavior of a
perfect fluid: frictional forces are negligible in this region. This
partitioning of the flow permits a mathematical solution to the problem of
flow about a body at high Reynolds numbers to be obtained. This is generally
done by first determining the flow about the body by the methods of classical
hydrodynamics---the so-called ``potential flow'' theory. The solution thus
obtained for the flow outside the boundary layer provides what mathematicians
call ``boundary conditions'' which allow the solution of the boundary-layer
equations to be carried out. The two solutions are then ``grafted together''
where the boundary layer ends and the outer flow begins, to provide a picture
of the total flow pattern.

The mathematical difficulties involved in the solution of a three-dimensional
boundary layer, as on a model rocket, are nevertheless of momentous
proportion. Several basic problems, such as the location of the ``transition
point'' where the character of the boundary-layer flow changes from
``laminar'' to ``turbulent'' (these terms will be discussed in detail later
on), as yet lack a firm theoretical foundation for their solution.
Furthermore, it is doubtful whether a complete mathematical description of the
flow pattern about his model would be of great benefit to the hobbyist, as
such a description can only be obtained by laborious numerical techniques
requiring the use of an electronic computer for each and every case to be
solved.

This section will therefore confine itself to a discussion of the basic
concepts of boundary-layer theory likely to be of greatest interest and
utility to the model rocketeer. The ultimate aim, of course, is to develop
expressions for the viscous stresses in the boundary layer which can be
related directly to the skin-friction drag. Fortunately, with the aid of
certain assumptions which will be enumerated as we go on, one \emph{can}
derive results which are applicable to the calculation of skin-friction drag
on model rockets.

\subsection{The Distinction Between Laminar and Turbulent Flow}\label{ch3:sec:3.2}

The fluid flow within a boundary layer may be characterized as either
\emph{laminar} or \emph{turbulent}. The distinction between these two states
may be observed in the stream of smoke rising from a burning cigarette in very
still air (Plate~\ref{ch3:plate:1}). For a short vertical distance the column
is narrow and straight, but above this region the flow disintegrates into a
disorderly, eddying stream. The smooth, unmixed phase of the flow is termed
\emph{laminar}; the rough, eddying part is called \emph{turbulent}.

\begin{plate}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth,height=0.7\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/ch3/plate01.jpg}
  \caption{Smoke rising from a cigarette in a still room. The path of the
  smoke is at first smooth and straight (laminar flow), but after it has risen
  for a certain distance it becomes rough and mixed (turbulent flow).}
  \label{ch3:plate:1}
\end{plate}

Whether the flow in the boundary layer along an object will be laminar or
turbulent depends to a large extent on the Reynolds number, although physical
conditions such as air turbulence and surface roughness can assume important
roles. Experimentally, the value of $R$ at which the transition from laminar
to turbulent flow in the boundary layer over a flat plate will occur has been
found to lie in the range between $3 \times 10^{5}$ and $3 \times 10^{6}$
(\ref{ch3:ref:15}). Since this interval includes the previously calculated
typical value of $R$ for a small model rocket, we may expect both laminar and
turbulent boundary-layer flows to exist on model rockets during flight,
depending on the velocity.

Consequently, we shall examine both types of boundary-layer flow. Because the
analysis for a three-dimensional body can be extremely complicated, our
discussion will be restricted to the steady, two-dimensional flow past a thin,
flat plate held parallel to the airstream. Although this is geometrically a
quite simple case, the skin-friction coefficients derived from the analysis of
the flat plate in laminar and turbulent flow form the basis for the
calculation of skin-friction drag on all three-dimensional shapes (assuming
that boundary-layer separation, discussed in Section~\ref{ch3:sec:4.2}, is not
present). The results of Sections~\ref{ch3:sec:3.3} and~\ref{ch3:sec:3.4} for
laminar and turbulent flow, respectively, will be used extensively later on in
this chapter for the determination of model rocket drag coefficients.

Next, in Section~\ref{ch3:sec:3.5}, a discussion of the mechanism of
boundary-layer transition is presented. Since laminar and turbulent boundary
layers produce markedly different skin-friction coefficients, even at
identical Reynolds numbers, it is important to know the location of the
transition zone in order to calculate viscous drag accurately. This problem,
unfortunately, has not as yet been well-researched for model rockets, and it
is necessary to assume a location for the transition. Section~\ref{ch3:sec:3.5}
analyzes the important factors affecting the location of the transition point
and discusses the calculation of the total skin-friction coefficient for
boundary layers exhibiting both laminar and turbulent regions.

Section~\ref{ch3:sec:3} will conclude with some estimates of the correction
factors required to apply the flat-plate results to three-dimensional
configurations. This will be seen to be small, usually 5\% or less, for the
fins and body of a model rocket.

\subsection{The Laminar Boundary Layer on a Smooth, Flat Plate}\label{ch3:sec:3.3}

In laminar flow, the fluid elements move parallel to the surface of the body
and there is little or no mixing of the fluid---i.e., transfer of
momentum---between adjacent streamlines. Historically, the first application
of Prandtl's boundary-layer theory was accomplished by H.~Blasius, who in 1908
computed the laminar boundary layer in uniform potential flow past a flat
plate. Since this example illustrates many of the important features of
boundary-layer analysis, we shall examine it in detail.

Steady fluid motion about such a plate is depicted in
Plate~\ref{ch3:plate:2} (\ref{ch3:ref:12}). The streamlines of the flow were
made visible by sprinkling aluminum particles on the water; the length of the
streaks left by the particles is proportional to their velocity. Very near the
surface of the plate, the traces are much shorter than in the exterior flow;
this region of reduced velocity is the boundary layer. Note also that the
apparent thickness of the boundary layer, which we shall shortly define in
mathematical terms, increases with distance downstream along the plate. This
is because the boundary-layer shearing stresses are retarding a greater volume
of fluid as distance from the leading edge increases.

Note that there is no indication of the absolute size of the plate in the
photograph. Hence we expect the velocity profiles (see
Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:13}) at all $x$-stations on the plate to be mathematically
similar; i.e., the dimensionless ratio $u/U_\infty$ may be expressed as some
function of the dimensionless coordinate ratio $y/\delta$, where $U_\infty$ is
the velocity of the exterior flow immediately outside the boundary layer
(calculated by potential-flow theory) and $\delta$ is the boundary-layer
thickness at any given location on the plate. This \emph{function} must be the
\emph{same} at all distances $x$ from the leading edge of the plate, although
$\delta$ itself becomes greater as $x$ increases.

  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/v2/ch3/fig13.pdf}

  one side of a flat plate at zero angle of attack.}
  \label{ch3:fig:13}

We can make an estimate of the boundary-layer thickness $\delta$ by equating
the viscous and inertial forces within the boundary layer and solving for the
value of $\delta$ at which this condition occurs. As seen in
Section~\ref{ch3:sec:2.2}, the inertial forces in the fluid flowing near an
object of characteristic dimension $L$ are of the order $\rho U_\infty^2/L$.
The friction forces per unit volume in a boundary layer of thickness $\delta$
are of the order $\mu U_\infty/\delta^2$, as the velocity gradient
$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$ exists only within the thickness of the
boundary layer itself. We then obtain
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:35}
\frac{\mu U_\infty}{\delta^2} \sim \frac{\rho U_\infty^2}{L}
\end{equation}
Solving for the boundary-layer thickness $\delta$,
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:36}
\delta \sim \sqrt{\frac{\mu L}{\rho U_\infty}} = \sqrt{\frac{\nu L}{U_\infty}}
\end{equation}
In the case of the flat plate the characteristic length of interest is the
distance from the leading edge $x$. Substituting $x$ for $L$, we obtain the
boundary-layer thickness as
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:37}
\delta \sim \sqrt{\frac{\nu x}{U_\infty}}
\end{equation}

The thickness of the boundary layer is thus seen to vary with the square root
of the distance from the leading edge. The object of a mathematical solution
to this problem is to obtain the constant of proportionality in the expression
for $\delta$, and to determine functions for the boundary-layer velocity
profile and the skin-friction drag.

The equations of fluid flow for a general, two-dimensional boundary layer are
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
  + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}
  &= -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dp}{dx} + \nu\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}
  \label{ch3:eq:38}\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} &= 0
  \label{ch3:eq:39}
\end{align}
where $u$ and $v$ are the local components of velocity in the $x$- and
$y$-directions, respectively. The boundary conditions required to completely
define the solution to this set of equations are
\begin{alignat*}{3}
y &= 0\colon &\quad u &= v = 0 &\qquad&
  \text{(both the tangential and the normal velocity}\\
&&&&& \text{\phantom{(}components must vanish at the wall)}\\[1ex]
y &= \infty\colon & u &= U_\infty(x,t) &&
  \text{(the tangential velocity component must}\\
&&&&& \text{\phantom{(}be identical to that computed from potential-}\\
&&&&& \text{\phantom{(}flow theory at large distances from the wall)}
\end{alignat*}
Those readers who are interested in the derivation of these equations from the
complete Navier-Stokes equations for general fluid flow may consult
Schlichting (\ref{ch3:ref:15}).

The Blasius problem assumes a steady flow, so all derivatives with respect to
time will vanish. Furthermore, the potential flow cannot detect the existence
of a thin, flat plate held parallel to it; $U_\infty$ just remains constant at
the free-stream value far from the plate, and hence $dp/dx$ is zero (there is
no pressure gradient along the plate), as may be determined from Bernoulli's
equation. The boundary-layer equations are then seen to reduce to
\begin{align}
u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}
  &= \nu\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} \label{ch3:eq:40}\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} &= 0
  \label{ch3:eq:41}
\end{align}
and the boundary conditions become
\begin{alignat*}{3}
y &= 0\colon &\quad u &= v = 0\\
y &= \infty\colon & u &= U_\infty &\qquad&
  \text{(note that $U_\infty$ is now independent of $x$ and $t$)}
\end{alignat*}

To solve these equations Blasius introduced a new dimensionless variable
$\eta = \frac{y}{\delta}$, or, using the expression for the approximate
boundary-layer thickness from equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:37},
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:42}
\eta = y\sqrt{\frac{U_\infty}{\nu x}}
\end{equation}
A dimensionless \emph{streamfunction} $f(\eta)$ was also introduced, such that
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:43}
f(\eta) = \frac{\psi}{\sqrt{\nu x U_\infty}}
\end{equation}
where $\psi(x,y)$ is the streamfunction that had been well known to
potential-flow theorists of the 19th Century; it is related to the flow
velocities $u$ and $v$ in such a manner that it identically satisfies
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:39}:
\begin{subequations}\label{ch3:eq:44}
\begin{align}
u &= \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y} \label{ch3:eq:44a}\\
v &= -\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} \label{ch3:eq:44b}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
from which we see that
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} &= \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y\,\partial x}\\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} &= -\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x\,\partial y}
  = -\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y\,\partial x}\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}
  &= \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y\,\partial x}
  - \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y\,\partial x} \equiv 0
\end{align*}
Using equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:42} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:43} to substitute into the
expressions for the velocity components, Blasius obtained
\begin{align}
u &= \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}
  = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\eta}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}
  = U_\infty\frac{df(\eta)}{d\eta} = U_\infty f'(\eta) \label{ch3:eq:45}\\
v &= -\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}
  = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\nu U_\infty}{x}}\,(\eta f' - f) \label{ch3:eq:46}
\end{align}
The prime symbol (${}'$) is often used in calculus to denote a derivative;
hence $\frac{df(\eta)}{d\eta}$ is also written $f'(\eta)$, or simply $f'$.
Higher-order derivatives are denoted by multiple primes; hence
$\frac{d^2 f(\eta)}{d\eta^2}$ is equivalent to $f''$, etc.

From equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:45} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:46} it is possible to
obtain expressions for the velocity derivatives of equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:40}
in terms of the nondimensional vertical coordinate $\eta$ and the
nondimensional streamfunction $f$:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
  &= \frac{\partial u}{\partial\eta}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}
  = \left[U_\infty f''\right]\left[-\frac{\eta}{2x}\right] \label{ch3:eq:47}\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}
  &= \frac{\partial u}{\partial\eta}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}
  = \left[U_\infty f''\right]\sqrt{\frac{U_\infty}{\nu x}} \label{ch3:eq:48}\\
\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}
  &= U_\infty\sqrt{\frac{U_\infty}{\nu x}}\,
  \frac{\partial f''}{\partial\eta}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}
  = \frac{U_\infty^2}{\nu x}f''' \label{ch3:eq:49}
\end{align}
Substituting~\eqref{ch3:eq:45} through~\eqref{ch3:eq:49}
into~\eqref{ch3:eq:40}, we have
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:50}
-\frac{U_\infty^2}{2x}\eta f'f'' + \frac{U_\infty^2}{2x}(\eta f' - f)f''
  = \nu\frac{U_\infty^2}{x\nu}f'''
\end{equation}
which can be algebraically simplified to
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:51}
ff'' + 2f''' = 0
\end{equation}
with the boundary conditions
\begin{alignat}{2}
\eta &= 0\colon &\qquad& f = 0;\quad f' = 0 \label{ch3:eq:52}\\
\eta &= \infty\colon && f' = 1 \label{ch3:eq:53}
\end{alignat}

Now the advantage Blasius obtained by introducing the dimensionless vertical
coordinate and dimensionless streamfunction was that he was able to reduce
equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:40} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:41}, a nonlinear system of two
partial differential equations, to equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:51}, which is known
as an \emph{ordinary}, \emph{homogeneous}, \emph{nonlinear} differential
equation: ``ordinary'' because $f$ is a function of the single variable
$\eta$, ``homogeneous'' because the right side is zero, and ``nonlinear''
because the product of $f$ with $f''$ appears in the equation. Such an
equation is easier to solve than the original system of equations;
``easier'', however, does \emph{not} mean ``easy'', and a solution to
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:51} has never been obtained in closed form. Power
series methods have been used to obtain accurate solutions by numerical means,
but the procedure is rather complex and its mechanics are of little interest
to model rocketeers (readers desiring a presentation of the method are urged
to consult Reference~\ref{ch3:ref:15}). Table~\ref{ch3:tab:1} presents the
numerical tabulation obtained by L.~Howarth from his highly accurate solution
of equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:51}.

Notice that the free-stream velocity is attained \emph{asymptotically}; i.e.,
that the boundary layer shades gradually into the exterior flow, so that the
concept of ``boundary-layer thickness'' is ambiguous. The generally-accepted
definition is based on the distance from the wall where the velocity differs
by 1\% from $U_\infty$; i.e., where $u = 0.99U_\infty$. From
Table~\ref{ch3:tab:1} we see that this corresponds to $\eta \cong 5.0$; hence
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:54}
\delta = 5\sqrt{\frac{\nu x}{U_\infty}}
\end{equation}
Evaluating this expression for $U_\infty = 60$ meters/second (6000
centimeters/second) and $\nu = 1.495 \times 10^{-5}$ meter$^{2}$/second
(0.1495 cm$^{2}$/second), we obtain\ednote{The coefficients of
equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:55a} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:55b}, $3.10 \times 10^{-3}$ and
$3.10 \times 10^{-2}$, and the $3.22 \times 10^{-2}$ of
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:57} below do not follow from the values just stated.
With $U_\infty = 60$ meters/second and $\nu = 1.495 \times 10^{-5}$
meter$^{2}$/second, equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:54} gives
$\delta = 2.50 \times 10^{-3}\sqrt{x}$ ($x$ and $\delta$ in meters) and
$\delta = 2.50 \times 10^{-2}\sqrt{x}$ (in centimeters), and
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:56} gives $v_\infty = 2.59 \times 10^{-2}/\sqrt{x}$
meters/second; the printed coefficients correspond to
$\nu = 2.31 \times 10^{-5}$ meter$^{2}$/second. The stated $\nu$ is evidently
meant, since the Reynolds numbers quoted after equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:55b} are
computed with it; the thicknesses quoted there (.031, .098 and .310 cm., for
.025, .079 and .250 cm.) and the 0.322 meter/second after

Neither the errata nor the supplements correct this; the coefficients and the
values computed from them are kept as printed.}
\begin{subequations}\label{ch3:eq:55}
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:55a}
\delta = 3.10 \times 10^{-3}\sqrt{x}
\end{equation}
for $x$ and $\delta$ in meters, and
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:55b}

\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
for $x$ and $\delta$ in centimeters. At $x = 1$ cm., then, the boundary-layer
thickness $\delta$ is .031 cm.; at $x = 10$ cm.\ $\delta$ is .098 cm., while
at $x = 100$ cm.\ (1 meter) $\delta$ is .310 cm. The corresponding Reynolds
numbers based on $x$ are $4.02 \times 10^{4}$, $4.02 \times 10^{5}$, and
$4.02 \times 10^{6}$, respectively.

The transverse component of velocity, $v$, can also be determined from the
Blasius solution by substituting values for $f$ and $f'$ at each value of
$\eta$ desired into equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:46}. The variation of the
nondimensional quantity $\frac{v}{U_\infty}\sqrt{\frac{U_\infty x}{\nu}}$ with
$\eta$ is plotted in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:15}. As $\eta$ approaches infinity
$v$ does not vanish, but instead attains the asymptotic value
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:56}
v_\infty = 0.865\,U_\infty\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{xU_\infty}}
\end{equation}
Examining again the case of $U_\infty = 60$ meters/second, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:57}
v_\infty = \frac{3.22 \times 10^{-2}}{\sqrt{x}} \qquad \text{meters/second}
\end{equation}
where $x$ must be given in meters. Thus, at a point 1 cm.\ from the plate
leading edge (that is, 0.01 meter), the value of $v_\infty$ is 0.322
meter/second---insignificant, of course, compared with $U_\infty$ but still
nonzero. The nonzero value of the transverse velocity at the upper edge of the
boundary layer is explained by the fact that the increasing boundary-layer
thickness causes the airstream to be displaced from the wall as it flows along
it, creating a slight outward velocity component. This is \emph{not} the same
as the flow \emph{separating} entirely from the wall, a phenomenon that occurs
only in an ``adverse pressure gradient''; i.e., when the pressure in the
boundary layer is increasing in the direction of flow. The flat plate at zero
angle of attack, it will be recalled, exhibits a \emph{zero} pressure
gradient.

The Blasius solution enables us to determine the skin-friction drag on the
flat plate with a laminar boundary layer by specializing the equation for the
viscous drag on a surface in general, two-dimensional flow:
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:58}
D_v = b\int_{s=0}^{\ell} \tau_o\cos\phi\,ds
\end{equation}
where $\tau_o$ is the shearing stress at the wall, $b$ is the span of the
object (dimension transverse to the flow), and $\ell$ is distance measured
around the object's profile from front to rear. The notation of
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:58} is explained pictorially in
Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:16}. For a flat plate of negligible thickness the angle
$\phi$ is zero everywhere, so $\cos\phi = 1.0$. In such a case the
differential path length $ds$ is also equal to $dx$, so
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:59}
D_v = b\int_{x=0}^{\ell} \tau_o\,dx
\end{equation}

  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/v2/ch3/fig16.pdf}
  \caption{Notation used in determining the friction drag of an object of
  finite thickness in laminar flow.}
  \label{ch3:fig:16}
\end{figure}%
From equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:34} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:48}\ednote{The last
member of this display is printed $\alpha U_\infty\sqrt{U_\infty/\nu x}$,
without the factor $\mu$ that the middle member carries.
$\alpha\mu U_\infty\sqrt{U_\infty/\nu x}$ is evidently meant: $\alpha$ stands
for $f''(0)$ alone, as the next sentence says, and
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:61} restores the $\mu$. Neither the errata nor the
supplements correct this; the display is kept as printed.}
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:60}
\tau_o(x) = \mu\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)_{y=0}
  = \mu U_\infty\sqrt{\frac{U_\infty}{\nu x}}\cdot f''(0)
  = \alpha U_\infty\sqrt{\frac{U_\infty}{\nu x}}
\end{equation}
where the quantity $f''(0)$ has been represented by the symbol $\alpha$
(\emph{not} to be confused with angle of attack in this application). From
Table~\ref{ch3:tab:1}, $\alpha$ is found to be 0.332. Hence, the
skin-friction drag on one side of the plate is
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:61}
D_v = .332\,\mu b U_\infty\sqrt{\frac{U_\infty}{\nu}}
  \int_{x=0}^{\ell}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}}
  = .664\,b U_\infty\sqrt{\mu\rho\ell U_\infty}
\end{equation}
For both sides (the total friction drag), we have
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:62}
2D_v = 1.328\,b\sqrt{U_\infty^3\mu\rho\ell}
\end{equation}
The friction drag coefficient for laminar flow over a flat plate wetted on
both sides is thus
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:63}
C_f = \frac{2D_v}{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2 A} = \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{R_\ell}}
\end{equation}
where $A$, the lateral area of both sides of the plate, is $2b\ell$ and
$R_\ell$ is the Reynolds number based on the length of the plate \emph{if} the
plate is too short for transition to turbulent flow to occur anywhere on its
surface. If the plate is sufficiently long for transition to occur, $R_\ell$
is the Reynolds number based on the distance from the leading edge to the
transition zone and the coefficient computed according to
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:63} is valid only for that portion of the plate
forward of the transition region. Recalling our numerical example of
$U_\infty = 60$ meters/second suppose we compute the friction drag coefficient
of a typical model rocket fin with an average chord of 3 cm.\ (.03 meter).
For such a case we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:64}
C_f = \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{12.06 \times 10^{4}}} = .00382
\end{equation}
We must remember, however, that friction drag coefficients as originally
computed are based on wetted area---that is, total lateral surface area. To
use them in calculating the overall drag coefficient of a model rocket, one
must convert them to coefficients based on maximum body frontal area. This
procedure will be described later on.
