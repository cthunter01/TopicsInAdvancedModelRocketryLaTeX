\subsection{Dynamical Behavior at a Constant, Nonzero Roll Rate}\label{ch2:sec:3.2}

\subsubsection{Generalized Homogeneous Response}\label{ch2:sec:3.2.1}

The presence of a constant roll rate causes the pitching and yawing movements
of the rocket to become coupled (cf.\ Section~\ref{ch2:sec:2.3}), so that the
differential equations for pitch and yaw must be solved simultaneously. We
recall that the homogeneous differential equations for this case are
\begin{align*}
I_L\frac{d^2\alpha_X}{dt^2} + C_2\frac{d\alpha_X}{dt} + C_1\alpha_X
  + I_R\omega_Z\frac{d\alpha_Y}{dt} &= 0\\
I_L\frac{d^2\alpha_Y}{dt^2} + C_2\frac{d\alpha_Y}{dt} + C_1\alpha_Y
  - I_R\omega_Z\frac{d\alpha_X}{dt} &= 0
\end{align*}
Although the general form of the solutions to these equations is somewhat
similar to the solutions of the decoupled case, the complete solutions
themselves are a good deal more complicated. The most generally applicable
ones are given by
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:51}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_X &= A_1 e^{-D_1 t}\sin(\omega_1 t + \varphi_1)
  + A_2 e^{-D_2 t}\sin(\omega_2 t + \varphi_2)\\
\alpha_Y &= A_1 e^{-D_1 t}\cos(\omega_1 t + \varphi_1)
  + A_2 e^{-D_2 t}\cos(\omega_2 t + \varphi_2)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Now it is clear that the derivative formulae for those parts of
equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:51} which carry the subscript ``2'' will be identical
in form to those for the terms which are subscripted with a ``1''. I can
therefore save a great deal of writing if, for now, I leave off the subscripts
and just write down the derivatives of one term of each equation's right-hand
member:
\begin{align*}
\frac{d\alpha_X}{dt} &= -AD e^{-Dt}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)
  + A\omega e^{-Dt}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)\\
\frac{d^2\alpha_X}{dt^2} &= A(D^2 - \omega^2)e^{-Dt}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)
  - 2A\omega D e^{-Dt}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)\\
\frac{d\alpha_Y}{dt} &= -AD e^{-Dt}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)
  - A\omega e^{-Dt}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)\\
\frac{d^2\alpha_Y}{dt^2} &= A(D^2 - \omega^2)e^{-Dt}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)
  + 2A\omega D e^{-Dt}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)
\end{align*}
This amounts to pretending, for the present, that there is only one initial
amplitude, only one inverse time constant, only one angular frequency and only
one phase angle in the solution. Such an intentional error is perfectly
permissible, just as it was in deriving equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:25}, for the
mathematics of the derivation will later point out that there are in fact two
each of these quantities.

Upon substitution of the simplified derivative relations the first (yaw)
differential equation becomes
\begin{align*}
&I_L A(D^2 - \omega^2)e^{-Dt}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)
  - 2I_L A\omega D e^{-Dt}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)\\
&- I_R\omega_Z AD e^{-Dt}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)
  - I_R\omega_Z A\omega e^{-Dt}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)\\
&- C_2 AD e^{-Dt}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)
  + C_2 A\omega e^{-Dt}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)
  + C_1 A e^{-Dt}\sin(\omega t + \varphi) = 0
\end{align*}
Removing the common factor $Ae^{-Dt}$ we have, from the requirement that sine
terms sum independently to zero,
\begin{equation*}
I_L(D^2 - \omega^2) - I_R\omega_Z\omega - C_2 D + C_1 = 0
\end{equation*}
and from the requirement that cosine terms sum independently to zero,
\begin{equation*}
-2I_L\omega D - I_R\omega_Z D + C_2\omega = 0
\end{equation*}
Substituting the expressions for $\alpha$, $d\alpha/dt$, and
$d^2\alpha/dt^2$ into the second (pitch) equation gives\ednote{The final
``0'' of this display is cut off at the right edge of the 1973 page (only its
left part is visible); it is restored from the parallel yaw expression above.}
\begin{align*}
&I_L A(D^2 - \omega^2)e^{-Dt}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)
  + 2I_L A\omega D e^{-Dt}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)\\
&+ I_R\omega_Z AD e^{-Dt}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)
  - I_R\omega_Z A\omega e^{-Dt}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)\\
&- C_2 AD e^{-Dt}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)
  - C_2 A\omega e^{-Dt}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)
  + C_1 A e^{-Dt}\cos(\omega t + \varphi) = 0
\end{align*}
from which the cosine terms give us
\begin{equation*}
I_L(D^2 - \omega^2) - I_R\omega_Z\omega - C_2 D + C_1 = 0
\end{equation*}
and the sine terms require that
\begin{equation*}
2I_L\omega D + I_R\omega_Z D - C_2\omega = 0
\end{equation*}
Now the sine equation from the first differential equation is identical to the
cosine equation from the second, and the cosine equation from the first just
equals the negative of the sine equation from the second and is therefore
equivalent to it. This confirms that a coupled solution of the
form~\eqref{ch2:eq:51} does in fact exist and that we can proceed to solve for
$\omega$ and $D$.
From the last equation above we can obtain $D$ in terms of $\omega$ as
\begin{equation*}
D = \frac{C_2\omega}{2I_L\omega + I_R\omega_Z}
\end{equation*}
Substituting this expression for $D$ into the second to last equation above
yields
\begin{equation*}
\frac{I_L C_2^2\omega^2}{4I_L^2\omega^2 + 4I_L I_R\omega_Z\omega + I_R^2\omega_Z^2}
  - I_L\omega^2 - I_R\omega_Z\omega
  - \frac{C_2^2\omega}{2I_L\omega + I_R\omega_Z} + C_1 = 0
\end{equation*}
Multiplying the left and right sides of this equation by
$(4I_L^2\omega^2 + 4I_L I_R\omega_Z\omega + I_R^2\omega_Z^2)$, collecting
terms and dividing the resulting equation by a factor of $(-4I_L^3)$ to cast
the equation into a form having a coefficient of 1 for the highest power of
$\omega$ which appears (this is called \emph{normalizing}), we obtain
\begin{align*}
&\omega^4 + 2\frac{I_R}{I_L}\omega_Z\omega^3
  + \left[-\frac{C_1}{I_L} + \frac{C_2^2}{4I_L^2}
    + \frac{5}{4}\frac{I_R^2}{I_L^2}\omega_Z^2\right]\omega^2\\
&+ \left[-\frac{C_1}{I_L}\frac{I_R}{I_L}\omega_Z
    + \frac{C_2^2}{4I_L^2}\frac{I_R}{I_L}\omega_Z
    + \frac{I_R^3}{4I_L^3}\omega_Z^3\right]\omega
  - \frac{C_1}{4I_L}\frac{I_R^2}{I_L^2}\omega_Z^2 = 0
\end{align*}
Now this is a \emph{quartic} equation in $\omega$, in which all powers of
$\omega$ from the zeroth to the fourth are present. The solution of such
equations is generally quite difficult, but we can reduce the complexity
somewhat in this case by considering the \emph{physics} of the situation and
the \emph{limiting behavior} of the solutions, rather than just plowing ahead
with the formal mathematics. By ``limiting behavior'' I mean just this: should
$I_R\omega_Z$ become vanishingly small compared to $I_L$, the solution of the
coupled motion will be very nearly identical to the solution of the decoupled
case. A mathematician would say that the coupled solution \emph{approaches}
the decoupled solution \emph{in the limit} as $\frac{I_R\omega_Z}{I_L}$ goes to
zero, so that we \emph{recover} the solution of the decoupled case from the
\emph{more general} solution of coupled motion. Similarly, for the case in
which $C_1$ and $C_2$ become vanishingly small compared to $I_L$, we obtain
the so-called \emph{force-free precession} which is familiar to the designers
of gyroscopic instruments; the coupled solution approaches a force-free
precession in the limit as $\frac{C_1}{I_L}$ and $\frac{C_2}{I_L}$ go to zero.
I am going to try to put these properties to use after making the following
substitutions in order to simplify the algebra of the angular frequency
equation:
\begin{align*}
X &= \frac{I_R}{I_L}\\
Y &= -\frac{C_1}{I_L}\\
Z &= \frac{C_2^2}{4I_L^2}
\end{align*}
The equation for $\omega$ then becomes
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:52}
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:52a}
\begin{aligned}
&\omega^4 + 2X\omega_Z\omega^3
  + \left[Y + Z + \tfrac{5}{4}X^2\omega_Z^2\right]\omega^2\\
&+ \left[YX\omega_Z + ZX\omega_Z + \frac{X^3}{4}\omega_Z^3\right]\omega
  + \frac{Y}{4}X^2\omega_Z^2 = 0
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Now since this is a fourth-degree polynomial equation, we know that there are
four possible values of $\omega$ which will satisfy it (the \emph{roots} of
the equation). Let these values be $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$. The equation can
then be written as
\begin{equation*}
(\omega - A)(\omega - B)(\omega - C)(\omega - D) = 0
\end{equation*}
which, when multiplied out, becomes
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:52b}
\begin{aligned}
&\omega^4 - (A + B + C + D)\omega^3
  + \left[AB + CD + (A + B)(C + D)\right]\omega^2\\
&- \left[CD(A + B) + AB(C + D)\right]\omega + ABCD = 0
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
Now equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:52a} and~\eqref{ch2:eq:52b} are just different
representations of the \emph{same} equation. This being the case, the
coefficient of a given power of $\omega$ in equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:52a} must
be identical to the coefficient of that same power of $\omega$ in
equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:52b}. This allows us to write
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:53}
\begin{align}
A + B + C + D &= -2X\omega_Z \label{ch2:eq:53a}\\
\left[AB + CD + (A + B)(C + D)\right]
  &= \left[Y + Z + \tfrac{5}{4}X^2\omega_Z^2\right] \label{ch2:eq:53b}\\
\left[CD(A + B) + AB(C + D)\right]
  &= -\left[YX\omega_Z + ZX\omega_Z + \frac{X^3}{4}\omega_Z^3\right]
  \label{ch2:eq:53c}\\
ABCD &= \frac{Y}{4}X^2\omega_Z^2 \label{ch2:eq:53d}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

Equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:53} are sufficient to determine the roots $A$, $B$,
$C$, and $D$ in terms of the constants $X$, $Y$, and $Z$. This, however, would
be an extremely difficult task if done by formal mathematics alone. Instead I
am going to examine the limiting behavior of this particular dynamical
system---a model rocket---to see if I cannot find some analytical ``short
cut'' that will help me to \emph{guess} the roots.

For $\frac{I_R\omega_Z}{I_L} = 0$, equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:52a} becomes
\begin{equation*}
\omega^4 + (Y + Z)\omega^2 = 0
\end{equation*}
Factoring this expression, we obtain
\begin{equation*}
\omega^2(\omega^2 + Y + Z) = 0
\end{equation*}
Two of the roots are thus $\omega = 0$. The remaining two are
\begin{equation*}
\omega = \pm\sqrt{-(Y + Z)}
\end{equation*}
or
\begin{equation*}
\omega = \pm\sqrt{\frac{C_1}{I_L} - \frac{C_2^2}{4I_L^2}}
\end{equation*}
which are just cases of decoupled, underdamped oscillation.

For the limiting case $\frac{C_1}{I_L} = \frac{C_2}{I_L} = 0$, we have
$Y = Z = 0$ and equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:52a} becomes
\begin{equation*}
\omega^4 + 2X\omega_Z\omega^3 + \tfrac{5}{4}X^2\omega_Z^2\omega^2
  + \frac{X^3}{4}\omega_Z^3\omega = 0
\end{equation*}
Factoring gives us
\begin{equation*}
\omega\left(\omega^3 + 2X\omega_Z\omega^2 + \tfrac{5}{4}X^2\omega_Z^2\omega
  + \frac{X^3}{4}\omega_Z^3\right) = 0
\end{equation*}
One of the four roots is seen to be $\omega = 0$. The remaining factor is a
cubic equation and will therefore have three roots. Let these be denoted by
$A'$, $B'$, and $C'$. Then
\begin{equation*}
(\omega - A')(\omega - B')(\omega - C') = 0
\end{equation*}
which, when expanded, becomes\ednote{This equation and the three coefficient
equations that follow are corrected per the 1973 errata sheet. The 1973
printing wrote $A$, $B$ and $C$ without primes in these four equations:
$\omega^3 - (A + B + C)\omega^2 + [(A + B)C + AB]\omega - ABC = 0$,
$-(A + B + C) = 2X\omega_Z$, $(A + B)C + AB = \frac{5}{4}X^2\omega_Z^2$ and
$-ABC = \frac{X^3}{4}\omega_Z^3$.}
\begin{equation*}
\omega^3 - (A' + B' + C')\omega^2 + \left[(A' + B')C' + A'B'\right]\omega
  - A'B'C' = 0
\end{equation*}
Then, equating coefficients of like powers, we have
\begin{align*}
-(A' + B' + C') &= 2X\omega_Z\\
(A' + B')C' + A'B' &= \tfrac{5}{4}X^2\omega_Z^2\\
-A'B'C' &= \frac{X^3}{4}\omega_Z^3
\end{align*}
I can now exercise on a reduced scale the techniques which I shall have to
apply to solve the general quartic equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:52a}. The
\emph{sum} of the roots of the cubic equation must equal $-2X\omega_Z$, while
their \emph{product} must equal $-\frac{X^3}{4}\omega_Z^3$. From this I
conclude that $A'$, $B'$, and $C'$ each contain a factor of $X\omega_Z$. If I
attempt, reasoning from symmetry, to postulate three equal roots you can see
that the coefficient equations will not be satisfied. If, however, I postulate
\emph{two} identical roots and a different third root, the sum and product
conditions will allow me to write
\begin{align*}
A' &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2}\\
B' &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2}\\
C' &= -X\omega_Z
\end{align*}
These roots also satisfy the center coefficient equation and \emph{are}
therefore the roots of the cubic equation. I am now faced with the problem of
determining which of these roots are \emph{physical} (i.e., which can really
be observed in the physical universe) and which are ``spurious'' or
``extraneous'' roots not applicable to the physical problem being solved. In
order to ascertain the physical roots I return to the equation obtained from
the cosine terms of the pitch differential equation and set $C_1 = C_2 = 0$.
This sets $D$ to zero and leaves me with
\begin{equation*}
-I_L\omega^2 - I_R\omega_Z\omega = 0
\end{equation*}
from which I obtain the two roots
\begin{align*}
\omega &= 0\\
\omega &= -\frac{I_R}{I_L}\omega_Z
\end{align*}
The first value represents a static displacement in pitch and yaw, and is the
root already obtained by factoring the quartic. The second value represents
the force-free precessional angular frequency of the rocket. Both values
represent observable physical states of the rocket; in fact, for general
initial conditions a combination of \emph{both} states will occur. There will
thus be \emph{two} angular frequencies of oscillation associated with coupled
motion, and we must be prepared to accept two of the four roots of the general
quartic equation as physical possibilities. In the reduced case of $Y = Z = 0$
these two roots are $\omega = 0$ and root $C'$ of the cubic equation. Roots
$A'$ and $B'$ are non-physical and must be discarded.

Having thus obtained complete information as to the limiting behavior of
equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:52a}, I am prepared to postulate solutions to the
general quartic equation. Symmetry considerations and
equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:53a} motivate me to guess that the roots are of the
following form:
\begin{align*}
A &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} + a\\
B &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} - a\\
C &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} + b\\
D &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} - b
\end{align*}
You can see that these values identically satisfy
equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:53a}. Equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:53b} becomes
\begin{equation*}
\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{2} - (a^2 + b^2) + X^2\omega_Z^2
  = Y + Z + \tfrac{5}{4}X^2\omega_Z^2
\end{equation*}
from which we obtain
\begin{equation*}
b^2 = -(Y + Z) + \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4} - a^2
\end{equation*}
Equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:53c} now appears as
\begin{equation*}
\left[\left(\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4} - b^2\right)(-X\omega_Z)
  + \left(\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4} - a^2\right)(-X\omega_Z)\right]
  = -X\omega_Z\left[Y + Z + \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right]
\end{equation*}
and, upon substitution of the expression for $b^2$ obtained
in~\eqref{ch2:eq:53b}, is seen to be identically satisfied. The final
coefficient equation, \eqref{ch2:eq:53d}, becomes
\begin{equation*}
\left(\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4} - a^2\right)\left(\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4} - b^2\right)
  = Y\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}
\end{equation*}
Substituting for $b^2$ the expression obtained in~\eqref{ch2:eq:53b} we can
obtain, after some rearrangement,
\begin{equation*}
a^4 + \left[Y + Z - \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right]a^2
  - Z\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4} = 0
\end{equation*}
Now this is a special kind of quartic equation known as a \emph{biquadratic}
equation. It can be solved as a quadratic equation for $a^2$ to give
\begin{equation*}
a^2 = \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{8} - \frac{Y + Z}{2}
  \pm \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(Y + Z - \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right)^2
    + ZX^2\omega_Z^2}
\end{equation*}
Solving for $b^2$ as obtained in~\eqref{ch2:eq:53b}, we
have\ednote{The radical in this expression is printed without the factor
$\frac{1}{2}$ that the expression for $a^2$ above carries. Substituting that
expression into $b^2 = -(Y + Z) + X^2\omega_Z^2/4 - a^2$ gives
$\mp\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(Y + Z - X^2\omega_Z^2/4)^2 + ZX^2\omega_Z^2}$, and the
roots~\eqref{ch2:eq:54} carry the $\frac{1}{2}$. Neither the errata nor the supplements correct this; the expression is kept as printed.}
\begin{equation*}
b^2 = \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{8} - \frac{Y + Z}{2}
  \mp \sqrt{\left(Y + Z - \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right)^2 + ZX^2\omega_Z^2}
\end{equation*}
Now there is an option here in that we can choose the upper or lower sign for
the square root terms. Having chosen a sign for the square root in $a^2$,
however, we are obliged to choose the corresponding sign for that in $b^2$.
Choosing the upper sign in each case, we have the following expressions for
the complete roots of the quartic equation:
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:54}
\begin{aligned}
A &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{8} - \frac{Y + Z}{2}
  + \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(Y + Z - \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right)^2
  + ZX^2\omega_Z^2}}\\
B &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{8} - \frac{Y + Z}{2}
  + \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(Y + Z - \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right)^2
  + ZX^2\omega_Z^2}}\\
C &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{8} - \frac{Y + Z}{2}
  - \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(Y + Z - \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right)^2
  + ZX^2\omega_Z^2}}\\
D &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{8} - \frac{Y + Z}{2}
  - \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(Y + Z - \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right)^2
  + ZX^2\omega_Z^2}}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We must determine which of these give us the correct limiting behavior. This
is a rather tricky procedure in this case and must be carefully carried out
step by step if inconsistencies are to be avoided. For the case
$X\omega_Z = 0$, the roots assume the form
\begin{align*}
A &= \sqrt{-\frac{Y + Z}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{(Y + Z)^2}}\\
B &= -\sqrt{-\frac{Y + Z}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{(Y + Z)^2}}\\
C &= \sqrt{-\frac{Y + Z}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{(Y + Z)^2}}\\
D &= -\sqrt{-\frac{Y + Z}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{(Y + Z)^2}}
\end{align*}
In simplifying these results we must bear in mind always the following rule of
algebra: when a quantity is squared, and the square root of the number thus
obtained is taken, all knowledge of the algebraic sign of the original
quantity is \emph{irrevocably lost}. The final result of such a sequence of
operations is the \emph{absolute value} of the original quantity, which is by
definition a positive number. In our example here,
\begin{align*}
\sqrt{(Y + Z)^2} &= |Y + Z|\\
&\equiv \text{``the absolute value of $(Y + Z)$''}
\end{align*}
Now
\begin{align*}
|Y + Z| &= Y + Z \qquad \text{if } (Y + Z) > 0\text{, but}\\
|Y + Z| &= -(Y + Z) \qquad \text{if } (Y + Z) < 0
\end{align*}
In discussing statically-stable rockets, we are referring always to the case
in which $Y$ is negative (that is, $C_1$ is positive). If we further stipulate
that the motion be underdamped, we will always have $(Y + Z)$ negative. Then
\begin{equation*}
\sqrt{(Y + Z)^2} = -(Y + Z)
\end{equation*}
and the roots become
\begin{align*}
A &= \sqrt{-(Y + Z)}\\
B &= -\sqrt{-(Y + Z)}\\
C &= 0\\
D &= 0
\end{align*}
Thus $A$ and $B$ are the roots which correctly describe the motion of an
underdamped, statically-stable rocket in the limit of roll coupling equal to
zero. For the opposite limit, that of $Y = Z = 0$, we have
\begin{align*}
A &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{8}
  + \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(-\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right)^2}}\\
B &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{8}
  + \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(-\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right)^2}}\\
C &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{8}
  - \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(-\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right)^2}}\\
D &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{8}
  - \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(-\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right)^2}}
\end{align*}
Now in this case
\begin{equation*}
\sqrt{\left(-\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}\right)^2} = \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}
\end{equation*}
whereupon
\begin{align*}
A &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}}\\
B &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}}\\
C &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2}\\
D &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2}
\end{align*}
Also,
\begin{align*}
\sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}} &= \frac{X\omega_Z}{2}
  \qquad \text{if } \omega_Z > 0\text{, but}\\
\sqrt{\frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4}} &= -\frac{X\omega_Z}{2}
  \qquad \text{if } \omega_Z < 0
\end{align*}
so that if $\omega_Z > 0$,
\begin{align*}
A &= 0\\
B &= -X\omega_Z
\end{align*}
while if $\omega_Z < 0$,
\begin{align*}
A &= -X\omega_Z\\
B &= 0
\end{align*}
Thus $A$ and $B$ are the physical roots in the limit of force-free precession
as well as in the limit of decoupled motion. $C$ and $D$ are spurious roots
and must be discarded. The two angular frequencies $A$ and $B$ describe the
complete spectrum of dynamic behavior as we proceed continuously from motion
dominated by roll coupling to motion dominated by aerodynamic moments (except
in a few very special cases to be pointed out later).

From this point on I am going to refer to root $A$ as angular frequency
$\omega_1$ and to root $B$ as angular frequency $\omega_2$. The larger of the
two will be called ``the fast mode'', the smaller ``the slow mode''. If
$\omega_Z$ is positive, $\omega_1$ will be the slow mode and $\omega_2$ the
fast mode; if $\omega_Z$ is negative, the reverse will be true (recall that a
positive $\omega_Z$ means that the rocket is spinning clockwise as viewed from
astern).

I am also going to adopt the abbreviation
\begin{equation*}
\mathscr{F} = \frac{X^2\omega_Z^2}{4} - (Y + Z)
\end{equation*}
which may be written in terms of the rocket's dynamic constants as
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:55}
\mathscr{F} = \frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2} + \frac{C_1}{I_L}
  - \frac{C_2^2}{4I_L^2}
\end{equation}
Under this convention the two angular frequencies appear as
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:56}
\begin{align}
\omega_1 &= -\frac{I_R\omega_Z}{2I_L}
  + \sqrt{\frac{\mathscr{F}}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathscr{F}^2
    + \frac{C_2^2 I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^4}}} \label{ch2:eq:56a}\\
\omega_2 &= -\frac{I_R\omega_Z}{2I_L}
  - \sqrt{\frac{\mathscr{F}}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathscr{F}^2
    + \frac{C_2^2 I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^4}}} \label{ch2:eq:56b}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Since a complete description of the coupled motion of the rocket for general
initial conditions must contain both modes, each with its own initial
amplitude and its own phase angle, equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:51} must indeed be
the solution to the problem. Substituting $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$, in turn,
into the equation
\begin{equation*}
D = \frac{C_2\omega}{2I_L\omega + I_R\omega_Z}
\end{equation*}
obtained earlier, we obtain the values
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:57}
\begin{align}
D_1 &= \frac{C_2}{2I_L}\left(\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_1
  + \frac{I_R}{2I_L}\omega_Z}\right) \label{ch2:eq:57a}\\
D_2 &= \frac{C_2}{2I_L}\left(\frac{\omega_2}{\omega_2
  + \frac{I_R}{2I_L}\omega_Z}\right) \label{ch2:eq:57b}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
for the two inverse time constants. Note that these both reduce to
$C_2/(2I_L)$ for the case where the roll rate is zero.

The values of $A_1$, $A_2$, $\varphi_1$, and $\varphi_2$ are determined by
the four initial conditions, which are:
\begin{align*}
\alpha_{X0} &= \text{value of $\alpha_X$ at $t = 0$}\\
\alpha_{Y0} &= \text{value of $\alpha_Y$ at $t = 0$}\\
\Omega_{X0} &= \text{value of $d\alpha_X/dt$ at $t = 0$}\\
\Omega_{Y0} &= \text{value of $d\alpha_Y/dt$ at $t = 0$}
\end{align*}
From equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:51} and their time derivatives we have that
\begin{align*}
\alpha_{X0} &= A_1\sin\varphi_1 + A_2\sin\varphi_2\\
\alpha_{Y0} &= A_1\cos\varphi_1 + A_2\cos\varphi_2\\
\Omega_{X0} &= -A_1 D_1\sin\varphi_1 + A_1\omega_1\cos\varphi_1
  - A_2 D_2\sin\varphi_2 + A_2\omega_2\cos\varphi_2\\
\Omega_{Y0} &= -A_1 D_1\cos\varphi_1 - A_1\omega_1\sin\varphi_1
  - A_2 D_2\cos\varphi_2 - A_2\omega_2\sin\varphi_2
\end{align*}
This system of four independent equations in four unknowns is reduced by
eliminating terms between the equations one by one until explicit formulae for
the initial amplitudes and phase angles are obtained. Beginning this process
with the angular velocity equations, for instance, I can write
\begin{align*}
D_1\Omega_{X0} + \omega_1\Omega_{Y0}
  &= -A_1\sin\varphi_1(D_1^2 + \omega_1^2)
   - A_2\sin\varphi_2(D_1 D_2 + \omega_1\omega_2)
   + A_2\cos\varphi_2(\omega_2 D_1 - \omega_1 D_2)\\
D_2\Omega_{X0} + \omega_2\Omega_{Y0}
  &= -A_1\sin\varphi_1(D_1 D_2 + \omega_1\omega_2)
   - A_2\sin\varphi_2(D_2^2 + \omega_2^2)
   + A_1\cos\varphi_1(\omega_1 D_2 - \omega_2 D_1)
\end{align*}
Now if you substitute $\alpha_{Y0} - A_2\cos\varphi_2$ for
$A_1\cos\varphi_1$ in the second equation and subtract it from the first you
will get
\begin{equation*}
\left[\begin{aligned}
&\Omega_{X0}(D_1 - D_2) + \Omega_{Y0}(\omega_1 - \omega_2)\\
&+ \alpha_{Y0}(\omega_1 D_2 - \omega_2 D_1)
\end{aligned}\right]
=
\left[\begin{aligned}
&A_1\sin\varphi_1(D_1 D_2 + \omega_1\omega_2 - D_1^2 - \omega_1^2)\\
&+ A_2\sin\varphi_2(D_2^2 + \omega_2^2 - D_1 D_2 - \omega_1\omega_2)
\end{aligned}\right]
\end{equation*}
Multiplying the equation for $\alpha_{X0}$ by the factor
$(D_2^2 + \omega_2^2 - D_1 D_2 - \omega_1\omega_2)$ and subtracting the result
from \emph{this} equation results in the expression
\begin{equation*}
A_1\sin\varphi_1\left[\begin{aligned}
&2(D_1 D_2 + \omega_1\omega_2) - D_1^2\\
&- D_2^2 - \omega_1^2 - \omega_2^2
\end{aligned}\right]
=
\left[\begin{aligned}
&\Omega_{X0}(D_1 - D_2) + \Omega_{Y0}(\omega_1 - \omega_2)
  + \alpha_{Y0}(\omega_1 D_2 - \omega_2 D_1)\\
&+ \alpha_{X0}(D_1 D_2 + \omega_1\omega_2 - D_2^2 - \omega_2^2)
\end{aligned}\right]
\end{equation*}
from which we can obtain
\begin{equation*}
A_1\sin\varphi_1 = \left[\vcenter{\hbox{$\displaystyle\frac{\begin{aligned}
&\Omega_{X0}(D_1 - D_2) + \Omega_{Y0}(\omega_1 - \omega_2)
  + \alpha_{X0}(D_1 D_2 + \omega_1\omega_2 - D_2^2 - \omega_2^2)\\
&+ \alpha_{Y0}(\omega_1 D_2 - \omega_2 D_1)
\end{aligned}}{2(D_1 D_2 + \omega_1\omega_2) - D_1^2 - D_2^2
  - \omega_1^2 - \omega_2^2}$}}\right]
\end{equation*}
By a similar sequence of algebraic operations we can obtain the value of
$A_1\cos\varphi_1$:
\begin{align*}
\omega_1\Omega_{X0} - D_1\Omega_{Y0}
  &= A_1\cos\varphi_1(\omega_1^2 + D_1^2)
   + A_2\sin\varphi_2(\omega_2 D_1 - \omega_1 D_2)
   + A_2\cos\varphi_2(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2)\\
\omega_2\Omega_{X0} - D_2\Omega_{Y0}
  &= A_1\cos\varphi_1(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2)
   + A_1\sin\varphi_1(\omega_1 D_2 - \omega_2 D_1)
   + A_2\cos\varphi_2(\omega_2^2 + D_2^2)
\end{align*}
Substituting $\alpha_{X0} - A_2\sin\varphi_2$ for $A_1\sin\varphi_1$ and
subtracting,
\begin{equation*}
\left[\begin{aligned}
&\Omega_{X0}(\omega_1 - \omega_2) + \Omega_{Y0}(D_2 - D_1)\\
&+ \alpha_{X0}(\omega_1 D_2 - \omega_2 D_1)
\end{aligned}\right]
=
\left[\begin{aligned}
&A_1\cos\varphi_1(\omega_1^2 + D_1^2 - \omega_1\omega_2 - D_1 D_2)\\
&+ A_2\cos\varphi_2(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2 - \omega_2^2 - D_2^2)
\end{aligned}\right]
\end{equation*}
Multiplying $\alpha_{Y0}$ by
$(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2 - \omega_2^2 - D_2^2)$ and subtracting the
result from the above gives us
\begin{equation*}
\left[\begin{aligned}
&\Omega_{X0}(\omega_1 - \omega_2) + \Omega_{Y0}(D_2 - D_1)\\
&+ \alpha_{X0}(\omega_1 D_2 - \omega_2 D_1)
  + \alpha_{Y0}(\omega_2^2 + D_2^2 - \omega_1\omega_2 - D_1 D_2)
\end{aligned}\right]
= A_1\cos\varphi_1\left[\begin{aligned}
&\omega_1^2 + D_1^2 + \omega_2^2 + D_2^2\\
&- 2(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2)
\end{aligned}\right]
\end{equation*}
Multiplying both sides of this by $(-1)$ and performing the appropriate
division, we obtain
\begin{equation*}
A_1\cos\varphi_1 = \left[\vcenter{\hbox{$\displaystyle\frac{\begin{aligned}
&\Omega_{X0}(\omega_2 - \omega_1) + \Omega_{Y0}(D_1 - D_2)
  + \alpha_{X0}(\omega_2 D_1 - \omega_1 D_2)\\
&+ \alpha_{Y0}(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2 - \omega_2^2 - D_2^2)
\end{aligned}}{2(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2) - D_1^2 - D_2^2
  - \omega_1^2 - \omega_2^2}$}}\right]
\end{equation*}
Dividing $A_1\sin\varphi_1$ by $A_1\cos\varphi_1$ and taking the inverse
tangent function of both sides of the resulting equation will give us an
explicit formula for $\varphi_1$ in terms of the initial conditions, angular
frequencies, and inverse time constants:
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:58}
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:58a}
\varphi_1 = \arctan\left[\frac{\begin{aligned}
&\Omega_{X0}(D_1 - D_2) + \Omega_{Y0}(\omega_1 - \omega_2)
  + \alpha_{X0}(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2 - \omega_2^2 - D_2^2)\\
&+ \alpha_{Y0}(\omega_1 D_2 - \omega_2 D_1)
\end{aligned}}{\begin{aligned}
&\Omega_{X0}(\omega_2 - \omega_1) + \Omega_{Y0}(D_1 - D_2)
  + \alpha_{X0}(\omega_2 D_1 - \omega_1 D_2)\\
&+ \alpha_{Y0}(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2 - \omega_2^2 - D_2^2)
\end{aligned}}\right]
\end{equation}
$\varphi_2$ is now computed from the relations
\begin{align*}
A_2\sin\varphi_2 &= \alpha_{X0} - A_1\sin\varphi_1\\
A_2\cos\varphi_2 &= \alpha_{Y0} - A_1\cos\varphi_1
\end{align*}
This time, since $A_1\sin\varphi_1$ and $A_1\cos\varphi_1$ are both
quantities which we have already computed for use in finding $\varphi_1$, we
can immediately write
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:58b}
\varphi_2 = \arctan\left[\frac{\alpha_{X0} - A_1\sin\varphi_1}
  {\alpha_{Y0} - A_1\cos\varphi_1}\right]
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
Both phase angles are now known and their sine and cosine functions can be
determined from trigonometric tables. Since we also know the explicit formulae
for $A_1\sin\varphi_1$ and $A_2\sin\varphi_2$, we can compute the initial
amplitudes as follows:
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:59}
\begin{align}
A_1 &= \frac{A_1\sin\varphi_1}{\sin\varphi_1} \label{ch2:eq:59a}\\
A_2 &= \frac{A_2\sin\varphi_2}{\sin\varphi_2} \label{ch2:eq:59b}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

Equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:51} and~\eqref{ch2:eq:55} through~\eqref{ch2:eq:59}
contain all the information necessary to completely describe the angular
oscillations of a rolling model rocket in pitch and yaw. For general initial
conditions, both the pitching and yawing motions are sums of two different
exponentially-damped sinusoids. The appearance of the motion is generally
quite complicated. The slower mode sets the basic pattern: the nose of the
rocket describes an inward spiral toward the position of zero deflection if
the rocket is statically stable, an outward spiral if it is statically
unstable, and a circle of constant radius if either (1) the corrective moment
coefficient $C_1$ is zero, or (2) the corrective moment coefficient is
positive but the damping moment coefficient $C_2$ is zero. The faster mode may
impose intricate secondary motions called \emph{nutations} upon the basic
pattern if it is sufficiently high in frequency and its amplitude is small. As
in the case of decoupled motion, however, the characteristics of the
oscillations are dependent upon the relative values of the rocket's inertial
and aerodynamic constants.

For the case of zero damping ($C_2 = 0$) the function $\mathscr{F}$ becomes
\begin{equation*}
\mathscr{F} = \frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2} + \frac{C_1}{I_L}
\end{equation*}
From which we have
\begin{align*}
\omega_1 &= -\frac{I_R\omega_Z}{2I_L}
  + \sqrt{\frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2} + \frac{C_1}{I_L}}\\
\omega_2 &= -\frac{I_R\omega_Z}{2I_L}
  - \sqrt{\frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2} + \frac{C_1}{I_L}}
\end{align*}
as long as $\mathscr{F}$ is positive, which it certainly will be for
statically-stable rockets. Since $C_2$ is zero, $D_1$ and $D_2$ will both be
zero and the expressions describing the motion become
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:60}
\begin{align}
\alpha_X &= A_1\sin(\omega_1 t + \varphi_1)
  + A_2\sin(\omega_2 t + \varphi_2) \label{ch2:eq:60a}\\
\alpha_Y &= A_1\cos(\omega_1 t + \varphi_1)
  + A_2\cos(\omega_2 t + \varphi_2) \label{ch2:eq:60b}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Both pitch and yaw are the sum of two simple harmonic motions. The
oscillations do not decay; they persist indefinitely at undiminished
amplitudes as shown in Figure~\ref{ch2:fig:26}. Needless to say, this
condition is undesirable; as in the decoupled case, the restoration of the
rocket to a position in which it is facing directly along the intended flight
path never occurs. Since damping is always present in some degree, however
slight, this case will not be literally observed for any real rocket. But, as
in decoupled motion, too little damping can result in an oscillation that
continues for an undesirably long time.

  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/v2/ch2/fig26.pdf}
  \caption{Undamped homogeneous response to general initial conditions in yaw
  and pitch of a model rocket spinning about its longitudinal axis at a nonzero
  roll rate $\omega_Z$. The relation of the initial conditions to the
  properties of the response has been illustrated. The pattern of pitching and
  yawing motions shown repeats itself periodically for all time, as there is
  no damping to dissipate the angular momentum of the oscillations.}
  \label{ch2:fig:26}
\end{figure}

For nonzero damping such that
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\mathscr{F}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathscr{F}^2
  + \frac{C_2^2 I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^4}} > \frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2}
\end{equation*}
coupled, positively-stable motion described by the complete forms of
equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:51} and~\eqref{ch2:eq:55}
through~\eqref{ch2:eq:59} will occur. The angular frequency of the fast mode
will be opposite in sign to that of the slow mode: the fast mode will be of
opposite sign to the roll rate, while the sign of the slow mode will be the
same as that of the roll rate. The damping coefficients $D_1$ and $D_2$ will
both be positive, meaning that the amplitudes of both modes will \emph{decay}
exponentially with time. The inverse time constant associated with the slow
mode will be smaller in magnitude than that associated with the fast mode; the
slow mode will therefore be a \emph{more slowly decaying} mode than the fast
mode. The fast mode decays more rapidly than a decoupled oscillation with the
same values of $C_1$, $C_2$, and $I_L$, while the slow mode decays more slowly
than such a decoupled oscillation. As a practical matter this means that the
slow mode will be the most important part of the oscillation after a
sufficient time has elapsed, so that roll coupling serves to \emph{reduce the
effectiveness of damping}. A representative case of coupled,
positively-stable motion is shown in Figure~\ref{ch2:fig:27}. It is in this
range of relative values of $C_1$, $C_2$, and $I_L$ that the designer wants
his model rocket to lie; under no other conditions do the oscillations
subside, whether the rocket is rolling or not.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/v2/ch2/fig27.pdf}
  \caption{Characteristic response of a model rocket with finite damping and
  nonzero roll rate to general initial conditions in yaw and pitch, showing
  the relation of the initial conditions to the properties of the response.
  Both the yawing and pitching oscillations eventually decay to zero and the
  model regains straight and true flight.}
  \label{ch2:fig:27}
\end{figure}

The condition which must be satisfied for roll-coupled characteristic motion
to be positively stable can be made more explicit by solving the inequality
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\mathscr{F}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathscr{F}^2
  + \frac{C_2^2 I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^4}} > \frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2}
\end{equation*}
for $\mathscr{F}$. We can express this relation as
\begin{equation*}
\sqrt{\mathscr{F}^2 + \frac{C_2^2 I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^4}}
  > \frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{2I_L^2} - \mathscr{F}
\end{equation*}
Squaring both sides and collecting terms, we have
\begin{equation*}
\frac{C_2^2 I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^4}
  > \frac{I_R^4\omega_Z^4}{4I_L^4} - \mathscr{F}\cdot\frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{I_L^2}
\end{equation*}
from which it can be seen that
\begin{equation*}
\mathscr{F} > \frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2} - \frac{C_2^2}{4I_L^2}
\end{equation*}
Upon examining the original expression for $\mathscr{F}$ given in
equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:55}, we see that this is just the requirement that
\begin{equation*}
C_1 > 0
\end{equation*}
\emph{Positive static stability is thus required for positively stable
characteristic motion, whether or not the rocket is spinning about its
centerline.} As the value of
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\mathscr{F}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathscr{F}^2
  + \frac{C_2^2 I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^4}}
\end{equation*}
decreases toward $\frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2}$ (that is, as the corrective
moment coefficient decreases toward zero), the angular frequency of the fast
mode approaches a value of $-\frac{I_R\omega_Z}{I_L}$ while that of the slow
mode approaches zero from above if $\omega_Z$ is positive, from below if
$\omega_Z$ is negative. The inverse time constant of the fast mode approaches
$C_2/I_L$, or twice the damping of the decoupled oscillation, but that of the
\emph{slow} mode \emph{approaches zero}. At $C_1 = 0$ the rocket remains
deflected indefinitely; it is neutrally stable, just as if it were not
rolling. As $C_1$ becomes \emph{negative}, so that
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\mathscr{F}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathscr{F}^2
  + \frac{C_2^2 I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^4}} < \frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2}
\end{equation*}
the inverse time constant of the slow mode \emph{also becomes negative}: the
angular deflection of the rocket from its intended direction of flight
\emph{increases with time}. A rocket with a negative corrective moment
coefficient is unstable, as emphasized above, whether it is rolling or not.

There is no fundamental change in the character of the rocket's motion as
damping gains ascendancy over corrective moment, as there is in the case of
decoupled motion, but the effect of too much damping is every bit as serious
in the case of roll-coupled oscillation as it is for decoupled response. The
condition of critical damping for rockets whose roll rate is zero, you may
remember, is
\begin{equation*}
\frac{C_1}{I_L} = \frac{C_2^2}{4I_L^2}
\end{equation*}
Under this condition $\mathscr{F}$ becomes simply
$\frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2}$, and
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\mathscr{F}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathscr{F}^2
  + \frac{C_2^2 I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^4}}
  = \frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{8I_L^2}
  + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2}
    \left(\frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2} + \frac{C_2^2}{I_L^2}\right)}
\end{equation*}
Now
\begin{equation*}
\frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2} + \frac{C_2^2}{I_L^2}
  > \frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2}
\end{equation*}
and therefore
\begin{equation*}
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2}
  \left(\frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2} + \frac{C_2^2}{I_L^2}\right)}
  > \frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{8I_L^2}
\end{equation*}
It follows that
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\mathscr{F}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathscr{F}^2
  + \frac{C_2^2 I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^4}} > \frac{I_R^2\omega_Z^2}{4I_L^2}
\end{equation*}
so that the motion \emph{is} stable, no matter how great the value of $C_2$.
This, of course, is consistent with our result that the value of $C_1$ alone
determines the stability of the oscillation. But the slow mode, you will
recall, will have an inverse time constant whose value is less than that of
the inverse time constant of an identical rocket which is not rolling;
therefore \emph{the same condition which produces critical damping in a
non-rolling rocket will, in an identical rocket which is rolling, produce an
oscillation that decays more slowly than a critically-damped response}. The
condition
\begin{equation*}
\frac{C_2^2}{4I_L^2} > \frac{C_1}{I_L}
\end{equation*}
which produces overdamping in rockets which are not rolling, will aggravate
this undesirable state of affairs, making the rate at which the deflections
subside even less. Deflection amplitudes which are \emph{large} and which
persist for \emph{long periods of time} can thus result from the same
conditions which produce critically-damped or overdamped behavior in
non-rolling rockets, so it remains good design practice to keep
\begin{equation*}
\frac{C_1}{I_L} > \frac{C_2^2}{4I_L^2}
\end{equation*}
for any model rocket, rolling or not.
