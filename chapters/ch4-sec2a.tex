\section{The Non-Oscillating Rocket: Solutions for Vehicles Launched Vertically}\label{ch4:sec:2}

\subsection{The Specialized Differential Equation for the Vertical Case}\label{ch4:sec:2.1}

In attempting to solve equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:16} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:17} we will introduce certain
physical and mathematical approximations that will enable the modeler to obtain solutions for a
number of specific, \emph{idealized} cases. Once these solutions are obtained, they will be modified
to account for the varying angle of attack produced by rigid-body motion (that is, pitching and
yawing oscillations). For most of the cases under consideration, it will be found possible to
obtain closed-form solutions to~\eqref{ch4:eq:16} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:17} while maintaining a high
order of accuracy.

The simplest idealizations of~\eqref{ch4:eq:16} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:17} that are consistent with the
accurate representation of an actual model rocket flight involve the assumptions that
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item The rocket is launched vertically and maintains a purely vertical direction throughout the
  course of its flight;
  \item The angle of attack is zero; that is, the rocket does not oscillate; and
  \item The mass of the rocket remains constant at the average of the initial mass and the burnout
  mass.
\end{enumerate}
With these approximations, equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:17} becomes identically zero and
equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:16} may be written in the form
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:18}
m\frac{dv}{dt} = F(t) - mg - kv^{2}
\end{equation}
where it is to be understood that $v$ is identical to $v_y$ in all cases of purely vertical flight.
Even this greatly simplified equation cannot be solved exactly in closed form. One can, however,
obtain excellent approximations by any of the procedures described in the following sections.

\subsubsection{Fehskens-Malewicki Solution}\label{ch4:sec:2.1.1}

This method consists of replacing $F(t)$ by the average value of the thrust over the burn time and
solving the resulting equation by a method known to mathematicians as \emph{separation of
variables}. This technique involves rearranging the differential equation algebraically so that
the dependent variable---velocity in this case---appears on one side of the equation and the
independent variable---time---appears on the other:
\begin{subequations}\label{ch4:eq:19}
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:19a}
\frac{m\,dv}{F - mg - kv^{2}} = dt
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:19a} can then be integrated to yield the value of the burnout velocity as
follows:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:19b}
\int_{0}^{v_b}\frac{m\,dv}{F - mg - kv^{2}} = \int_{0}^{t_b} dt
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
Upon performing the integrations one obtains
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:20}
v_b = \sqrt{\frac{F - mg}{k}}\,\tanh\Bigl[\tfrac{t_b}{m}\sqrt{k(F - mg)}\Bigr]
\end{equation}
where $F$ denotes the average thrust, $v_b$ is the burnout velocity, and $t_b$ is the engine burn
time. Equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:20} was first obtained by Leonard G. Fehskens and later,
independently, by Douglas J. Malewicki. Both researchers also performed an additional integration
with respect to time to obtain the burnout altitude, $y_b$, in the form
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:21}
y_b = \frac{m}{k}\ln\Bigl[\cosh\Bigl(\tfrac{t_b}{m}\sqrt{k(F - mg)}\Bigr)\Bigr]
\end{equation}
In equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:20} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:21} the notations $\tanh(\ )$, $\ln(\ )$, and
$\cosh(\ )$ refer to the hyperbolic tangent, natural logarithm, and hyperbolic cosine functions of
the parenthesized quantity, respectively. These functions, like the natural trigonometric
functions, have been tabulated and may be found in any good book of mathematical tables and
formulae. In order to use such tables, as with the natural trigonometric functions, it is
necessary to compute the numerical value of the parenthesized quantity, or \emph{argument}, of the
function beforehand. The numerical value of the argument is then located in the table and matched
with the corresponding value of the desired function.

\subsubsection{Caporaso-Bengen Solution}\label{ch4:sec:2.1.2}

A second approximate procedure for calculating the burnout velocity and burnout altitude consists
in trying to integrate~\eqref{ch4:eq:18} directly with respect to time. In this method the actual,
time-varying value of $F(t)$ can be used but the integral of $v^{2}$ with respect to time must be
approximated:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:22}
\int_{0}^{t_b} m\frac{dv}{dt}\,dt = \int_{0}^{t_b} F(t)\,dt - \int_{0}^{t_b} mg\,dt
  - \int_{0}^{t_b} kv^{2}\,dt
\end{equation}
The left-hand side of~\eqref{ch4:eq:22} becomes the burnout velocity times the average mass. The
thrust integral just yields the total impulse of the engine, irrespective of the particular
functional form of $F(t)$, and the weight term is just the product of the average weight $mg$ and
the burn time $t_b$. The drag integral, however, must be computed by considering $v^{2}$ as its
equivalent form $v(dy/dt)$ and applying the following rule of integral calculus:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:23}
\int_{0}^{t_b} kv\frac{dy}{dt}\,dt = kyv\Big]_{0,0}^{y_b,v_b} - k\int_{0}^{t_b} y\frac{dv}{dt}\,dt
\end{equation}
The first term on the right of~\eqref{ch4:eq:23} is just the product $kyv$ taken at $v = v_b$ and
$y = y_b$, minus the product $kyv$ taken at $v = 0$ and $y = 0$---or simply $ky_bv_b$. The second
term, however, cannot in general be integrated. The integral on the left side of~\eqref{ch4:eq:23}
is therefore \emph{truncated} by disregarding the second term on the right to obtain a first
approximation. The approximate solution of~\eqref{ch4:eq:22} then becomes
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:24}
mv_b = I_t - mgt_b - ky_bv_b
\end{equation}
where $I_t$ denotes the total impulse of the motor. Equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:24} can then be
integrated again if one replaces the velocities, times, and altitudes by ``dummy'' running
variables to avoid confusing their running values with their end-point values. Upon completion of
the integration one obtains\ednote{The inner integral of equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:25} is printed
with the upper limit $t_b$, like the outer one. The running time of the outer integration is
evidently meant, as the ``dummy'' running variables of this sentence imply: with $t_b$ the inner
integral is the constant $I_t$ and the first term is $I_tt_b$, which does not depend on the
functional form of the thrust (the text goes on to say that the burnout altitude does), and a
constant thrust $F$ would give $Ft_b^{2}$ where equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:26} has $Ft_b^{2}/2$. Neither the errata nor the
supplements correct this; the limit is kept as printed.}
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:25}
my_b = \int_{0}^{t_b}\Bigl[\int_{0}^{t_b} F(t)\,dt\Bigr]\,dt - mg\frac{t_b^{2}}{2} - \frac{ky_b^{2}}{2}
\end{equation}
where you should note that, once the drag integral~\eqref{ch4:eq:23} has been truncated to
obtain~\eqref{ch4:eq:24}, no further truncation is required to obtain~\eqref{ch4:eq:25}. It can be
seen that, while the burnout \emph{velocity} does not depend on the exact functional form of the
thrust, the \emph{burnout altitude does}. In particular, if we approximate $F(t)$ by a constant,
average value $F$ as in equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:19}, \eqref{ch4:eq:25} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:26}
my_b = F\frac{t_b^{2}}{2} - mg\frac{t_b^{2}}{2} - \frac{ky_b^{2}}{2}
\end{equation}
which, when solved algebraically for $y_b$, yields
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:27}
y_b = \frac{-m + \sqrt{m^{2} + kt_b^{2}(F - mg)}}{k}
\end{equation}
Upon substituting~\eqref{ch4:eq:27} into~\eqref{ch4:eq:24} and solving for $v_b$ one obtains
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:28}
v_b = \frac{I_t - mgt_b}{\sqrt{m^{2} + kt_b^{2}(F - mg)}}
\end{equation}
This alternative approximate approach was first used by William P. Bengen and the present author
shortly after Fehskens had derived equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:20} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:21}.

\subsubsection{Caporaso-Riccati Solution}\label{ch4:sec:2.1.3}

A third solution to equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:18} can be obtained by recognizing~\eqref{ch4:eq:18} as
a form of what mathematicians call the Riccati equation. The method of Riccati can then be applied
to solve~\eqref{ch4:eq:18} as follows:

Suppose we introduce the transformation of variables
\begin{equation*}
v = \frac{m}{k}\frac{\dot{u}}{u}
\end{equation*}
and substitute $\frac{m}{k}\frac{\dot{u}}{u}$ for $v$ everywhere in~\eqref{ch4:eq:18}. The result
will be a second-order \emph{linear} differential equation for the unknown function $u$:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:29}
\frac{m^{2}}{k}\biggl[\frac{\ddot{u}}{u} - \Bigl(\frac{\dot{u}}{u}\Bigr)^{2}\biggr]
  + \frac{m^{2}}{k}\Bigl(\frac{\dot{u}}{u}\Bigr)^{2} = F(t) - mg
\end{equation}
Combining terms, we have
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:30}
\frac{m}{k}\frac{\ddot{u}}{u} = \frac{F(t) - mg}{m}
\end{equation}
Upon multiplication of both sides by $u$ and a little rearrangement, a linear, second-order
differential equation for $u$ with one time-varying coefficient is obtained:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:31}
\frac{m}{k}\ddot{u} - \biggl[\frac{F(t) - mg}{m}\biggr]u = 0
\end{equation}
Thus far, it has not been necessary to make any approximations; one can obtain
equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:31} using the full, general functional form of $F(t)$. In fact, the Riccati
technique would even have permitted the use of the time-varying mass $m(t)$ instead of the average
value $m$.

Equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:31}, however, is impossible to solve in closed form unless it is simplified
in some manner. It \emph{will} possess a relatively simple and rather well-known solution if all
the coefficients of the various derivatives of $u$ are constants. In order to obtain and
investigate this solution we shall, in addition to approximating $m(t)$ by the average mass $m$,
consider $F(t)$ to be $F$, the average value of the thrust over the burn time. Under these
assumptions~\eqref{ch4:eq:31} has a solution familiar to students of mathematics and the sciences,
namely the exponential solution
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:32}
u = A_1e^{Ht} + A_2e^{-Ht}
\end{equation}
where $e$ is the base of the natural, or Napierian, system of logarithms and $H$ has the following
form:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:33}
H = \frac{1}{m}\sqrt{k(F - mg)}
\end{equation}
The constants $A_1$ and $A_2$ must be determined from the \emph{initial conditions} that both the
velocity and the altitude of the model are zero at $t = 0$. Constructing $v$ from $u$ according to
the variable transformation $v = \frac{m}{k}\frac{\dot{u}}{u}$ results in
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:34}
v = \frac{m}{k}\biggl[\frac{A_1He^{Ht} - A_2He^{-Ht}}{A_1e^{Ht} + A_2e^{-Ht}}\biggr]
\end{equation}
Imposing the initial condition that the velocity is zero at $t = 0$ forces the numerator
of~\eqref{ch4:eq:34} to vanish for this value of time:
\begin{equation*}
A_1H - A_2H = 0
\end{equation*}
so that
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:35}
A_1 = A_2
\end{equation}
When $A_1$ is substituted for $A_2$ everywhere in~\eqref{ch4:eq:34} it cancels in division, leaving
us with
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:36}
v = H\frac{m}{k}\biggl[\frac{e^{Ht} - e^{-Ht}}{e^{Ht} + e^{-Ht}}\biggr]
\end{equation}
Now it is known that
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:37}
\frac{e^{Ht} - e^{-Ht}}{e^{Ht} + e^{-Ht}} = \tanh(Ht)
\end{equation}
for this is just the exponential definition of the hyperbolic tangent. The burnout velocity can
then be obtained by setting $t = t_b$ and substituting the right-hand side of
equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:33} for $H$:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:38}
v_b = \sqrt{\frac{F - mg}{k}}\,\tanh\Bigl[\tfrac{t_b}{m}\sqrt{k(F - mg)}\Bigr]
\end{equation}
The altitude is determined by integrating the expression for the velocity with respect to time.
When this is done it is found that the resulting equation identically satisfies the condition that
the altitude be zero at $t = 0$, and that the burnout altitude is given by
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:39}
y_b = \frac{m}{k}\ln\Bigl[\cosh\Bigl(\tfrac{t_b}{m}\sqrt{k(F - mg)}\Bigr)\Bigr]
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:38} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:39} are seen to be identical to
equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:20} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:21}. The Riccati technique is thus an alternative
procedure by which one can obtain the Fehskens-Malewicki solution.

Equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:39} concludes this section's treatment of model rocket trajectory analysis,
which has been restricted to the determination of the \emph{burnout velocity} and \emph{burnout
altitude} of non-oscillating, single-staged model rockets flying straight upward. For the benefit of
those readers who have found the mathematical derivations burdensome or impossible to follow, I
shall complete the section with a summary of the important results and explicit instructions on
how to apply them.

To use the \emph{Fehskens-Malewicki} method, compute the burnout velocity using
equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:20} and the burnout altitude using equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:21}.

To use the \emph{Caporaso-Bengen} method, compute the burnout velocity by using
equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:28} and the burnout altitude according to equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:27}.

To use the \emph{Caporaso-Riccati} method, compute the burnout velocity and burnout altitude
according to equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:38} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:39}, respectively---although, since
these equations are identical to those of the Fehskens-Malewicki method, there is no real reason to
consider them distinct from the results obtained by Fehskens and Malewicki.

\subsection{Extension of the Solutions to Multistaged Vehicles}\label{ch4:sec:2.2}

The problem of computing the burnout velocity and altitude of the upper stages of a multistaged
model rocket is somewhat more difficult than that of computing the burnout velocity and altitude of
a single-staged model. In each case the differential equations of motion have precisely the same
form; in fact, the general forms of the solutions are also identical. The crucial difference
between the single-stage and multistage solutions lies in the choice of \emph{initial conditions
and limits of integration}. The problem essentially reduces to one of choosing the correct limits
in the indefinite integral solutions thus far derived.

As will be shown in Section~\ref{ch4:sec:2.5}, the approximations derived in
Section~\ref{ch4:sec:2.1} are quite accurate and compare very well with each other, as well as with
computer solutions obtained by interval methods (which are, in the limit of vanishingly small
intervals, exact). Since extending the methods of Section~\ref{ch4:sec:2.1} to multistaged vehicles
is algebraically laborious, we shall first want to choose the easiest approximation to handle
analytically. Such a choice is perfectly justified, as all the approximations are more than
sufficiently accurate for model rocketry purposes.

\subsubsection{Extended Caporaso-Bengen Solution}\label{ch4:sec:2.2.1}

The most logical choice in this respect would seem to be the Caporaso-Bengen method used in
deriving equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:27} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:28}, since it not only is analytically
simple, but also affords the greatest physical insight into the problem of choosing the limits of
integration. In extending this method we shall first apply equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:22} to a
two-staged model, modifying the limits of integration for the calculation of the second-stage
burnout velocity and burnout altitude. The following notation will be used:\ednote{Equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:40}
and~\eqref{ch4:eq:44}--\eqref{ch4:eq:53} are corrected per the ``Summary: Corrections and additions to
altitude equations for 2-stage and multistage rockets'' (no author or date shown), which treats the
burning of an upper stage as an initial-value problem: with $\hat{t} = t - t_1$ and $\hat{y} = y - y_1$,

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=2.5em,label={}]
  \item $y_1$ = $y_b$ = burnout altitude of first stage
  \item $v_1$ = $v_b$ = burnout velocity of first stage
  \item $v_2$ = burnout velocity of second stage
  \item $y_2$ = altitude gained during the burning time of the second-stage engine (not the total
  second-stage burnout altitude)
  \item $m_1$ = average mass of first and second stages together
  \item $m_2$ = average mass of second stage alone
  \item $k_2$ = second-stage drag parameter
  \item $t_2$ = burning time of second-stage engine
  \item $t_1$ = $t_b$ = burning time of first-stage engine
  \item $v$ = velocity at any time during the burning of the second-stage engine
  \item $\hat{y}$ = altitude gained since the ignition of the second-stage engine (not the total
  altitude above the launch point)
  \item $\hat{t}$ = time elapsed since the ignition of the second-stage engine
  \item $I_{t2}$ = total impulse of the second-stage engine
\end{itemize}

To better conceptualize the reasons for the choice of limits to be made in the following
derivation, suppose we consider the rocket just after the burnout of the first stage, in the
infinitesimal time interval before the second stage ignites. The second stage has already
jettisoned the first through the action of the propellant-wall blow-through function of the
first-stage engine (which also ignites the second-stage engine), but the second-stage engine has
not been lit long enough to have developed any measurable thrust. The upper stage will have
aerodynamic drag on it equal to the square of the first-stage burnout velocity times the
second-stage drag parameter; i.e., the drag will equal $k_2v_1^{2}$. The weight of the second stage
is the only other force acting on the model and, if the second-stage engine did not ignite, would
(in conjunction with the drag) begin to decrease its upward momentum.

The second stage now fires and contributes to the vehicle a quantity of momentum equal to its total
impulse. Thus, after the burnout of the first stage, the momentum of the rocket is changed only by
the forces acting on it during the second-stage burning time and we may write\ednote{Equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:40}
is rewritten per the Summary, with the time $\hat{t}$ elapsed since second-stage ignition as the variable of
integration. The 1973 equation read $\int_{t_1}^{t_1+t_2} m_2\frac{dv}{dt}\,dt = \int_{t_1}^{t_1+t_2}
F_2(t)\,dt - \int_{t_1}^{t_1+t_2} m_2g\,dt - \int_{t_1}^{t_1+t_2} k_2v^{2}\,dt$. Equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:43},
which the Summary does not change, keeps the 1973 limits $t_1$ and $t_1 + t_2$.}
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:40}
\int_{0}^{t_2} m_2\frac{dv}{d\hat{t}}\,d\hat{t} = \int_{0}^{t_2} F_2(\hat{t})\,d\hat{t}
  - \int_{0}^{t_2} m_2g\,d\hat{t} - \int_{0}^{t_2} k_2v^{2}\,d\hat{t}
\end{equation}
The value of the integral on the left taken between the indicated limits is just $m_2(v_2 - v_1)$.
It is now necessary to make the same truncation approximation as was made in
equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:23}, and to supply the proper limits of integration for making such an
approximation. Here we are integrating the drag \emph{from a nonzero quantity up through its
second-stage burnout value}; we are attempting to find the drag integral only over the
second-stage burning time. Now it is a well-known property of integrals that they obey the
following additive relationship with respect to their limits of integration:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:41}
\int_{0}^{\alpha} f(x)\,dx = \int_{0}^{\beta} f(x)\,dx + \int_{\beta}^{\alpha} f(x)\,dx
\end{equation}
where $x$, in this case, represents any independent variable (not the horizontal coordinate to be
used in our later investigation of nonvertical trajectories), and $f(x)$ represents any function of
that variable. In the present case, we desire the solution for the component integral over the
last interval:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:42}
\int_{\beta}^{\alpha} f(x)\,dx = \int_{0}^{\alpha} f(x)\,dx - \int_{0}^{\beta} f(x)\,dx
\end{equation}
Replacing the dummy function $f(x)$ by $v^{2}$ and the dummy variable $x$ by $t$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:43}
\int_{t_1}^{t_1+t_2} v^{2}\,dt = \int_{0}^{t_1+t_2} v^{2}\,dt - \int_{0}^{t_1} v^{2}\,dt
\end{equation}
and using the same truncation procedure as in~\eqref{ch4:eq:23},\ednote{Equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:44}
to~\eqref{ch4:eq:47} are corrected per the Summary. The 1973 equations read (44)
$\int_{t_1}^{t_1+t_2} v^{2}\,dt = (y_1 + y_2)v_2 - y_1v_1$, (45) $m_2v_2 - m_2v_1 = F_2t_2 - m_2gt_2 -
k_2[(y_1 + y_2)v_2 - y_1v_1]$, (46) $v_2 = \bigl[t_2(F_2 - m_2g) + m_2v_1 + k_2y_1v_1\bigr]\big/\bigl[m_2
+ k_2(y_1 + y_2)\bigr]$ and (47) $m_2y_2 - m_2v_1t_2 = \frac{t_2^{2}}{2}(F_2 - m_2g) - k_2y_1y_2 -
k_2\frac{y_2^{2}}{2} + k_2y_1v_1t_2$; the Summary says that (47) was evaluated correctly except for the
terms in $y_1$. The left-hand side of the corrected~\eqref{ch4:eq:44} is the drag integral itself,
with $k_2v^{2}$ written as $k_2v\,d\hat{y}/d\hat{t}$, where the 1973 one was the integral of $v^{2}$
on the left of~\eqref{ch4:eq:43}. The 1973 sentence before~\eqref{ch4:eq:45} is kept although it
no longer matches: it evaluates the integrals ``subject to the approximations of constant average

Summary writes ``instead of $F_2t_2$ for $\int_{0}^{t_2} F_2(\hat{t})\,d\hat{t}$'' so that, as in the
single-stage case, the burnout velocity does not depend on the functional form of the thrust; the
Summary introduces the constant average thrust $F_2$ only at~\eqref{ch4:eq:47}.}
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:44}
\int_{0}^{t_2} k_2v\frac{d\hat{y}}{d\hat{t}}\,d\hat{t} = k_2\hat{y}v\Big]_{0,v_1}^{y_2,v_2}
  - k_2\int_{0}^{t_2}\hat{y}\frac{dv}{d\hat{t}}\,d\hat{t} \cong k_2y_2v_2
\end{equation}
It is essential to note here that we are integrating the drag \emph{as it would have been if the
drag parameter $k$ had been $k_2$ throughout the flight}. The reason for this is that, at the
instant after first-stage burnout and jettison, the drag is $k_2v_1^{2}$ and $k_1$ should have
nothing at all to do with the integral of the drag during the second-stage burn, except insofar as
it has played a role in determining $v_1$ (which is already known).

The drag integral must be evaluated in this manner, since there is considerable analytical
difficulty involved in matching the drag of the rocket just before and just after burnout and
jettison of the first stage without assuming that the drag parameter has been $k_2$ up to that
point. Substituting the approximation~\eqref{ch4:eq:44} into~\eqref{ch4:eq:40} and evaluating the
other integrals subject to the approximations of constant average thrust and mass, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:45}
m_2v_2 - m_2v_1 = I_{t2} - m_2gt_2 - k_2y_2v_2
\end{equation}
which can be solved to give $v_2$ in the form
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:46}
v_2 = \frac{I_{t2} - m_2gt_2 + m_2v_1}{m_2 + k_2y_2}
\end{equation}
Proceeding as in the single-stage case, we integrate~\eqref{ch4:eq:45} to obtain a solution for
$y_2$:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:47}
m_2y_2 - m_2v_1t_2 = \frac{t_2^{2}}{2}(F_2 - m_2g) - k_2\frac{y_2^{2}}{2}
\end{equation}
You should note at this point that all quantities relating to the first-stage burnout values $v_1$
and $y_1$ are to be treated as \emph{constants} and \emph{not} to be integrated as their respective
functions. The first-stage burnout velocity and altitude are assumed to be already known, having
been computed by treating the model as if it were single-staged and applying the methods of
Section~\ref{ch4:sec:2.1}. Equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:47} can then be solved for $y_2$,
yielding\ednote{Equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:48} to~\eqref{ch4:eq:51} are corrected per the Summary. The
1973 equations read (48) $y_2 = \bigl[-(m_2 + k_2y_1) + \sqrt{R_2}\,\bigr]\big/k_2$, (49) $v_2 =
\bigl[t_2(F_2 - m_2g) + m_2v_1 + k_2y_1v_1\bigr]\big/\sqrt{R_2}$, (50) $v_n = \bigl[t_n(F_n - m_ng) +
m_nv_{n-1} + k_ny_{n-1}v_{n-1}\bigr]\big/\sqrt{R_n}$ and (51) $y_n = -[m_n + k_n(y_1 + \ldots +
y_{n-1})]/k_n + \sqrt{R_n}\big/k_n$, where, writing the radicands here as $R_2$ and $R_n$, $R_2 = (m_2 +
k_2y_1)^{2} + 2k_2t_2[\tfrac{t_2}{2}(F_2 - m_2g) + (k_2y_1v_1 - m_2v_1)]$ and $R_n = [m_n + k_n(y_1 +
\ldots + y_{n-1})]^{2} + 2k_nt_n\{\tfrac{t_n}{2}(F_n - m_ng) + [k_n(y_1 + \ldots + y_{n-1})v_{n-1} -
m_nv_{n-1}]\}$. In~(51) the radicand is printed without its right-hand brace after the bracket that
follows $v_{n-1}$, which the 1973 errata sheet adds. The Summary does not define the $I_{tn}$ of
the corrected~\eqref{ch4:eq:50}, evidently the total impulse of the $n$th-stage engine; the sentence
after~\eqref{ch4:eq:51} defines $F_n$, $t_n$, $k_n$ and $m_n$ only. The 1973 prose is kept where it
no longer matches: the paragraph before~\eqref{ch4:eq:48} still says that $y_1$ is to be treated as
a constant, and the check after~\eqref{ch4:eq:49} still sets $y_1$ and $v_1$ to zero, although $y_1$ no longer appears (with $v_1 = 0$ the
corrected~\eqref{ch4:eq:48} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:49} do reduce to~\eqref{ch4:eq:27}
and~\eqref{ch4:eq:28}). After~\eqref{ch4:eq:49} the Summary asks for the discussion of limiting
behavior to be revised to eliminate the reference to $y_1$, but gives no text.}
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:48}
y_2 = \frac{-m_2 + \sqrt{m_2^{2} + 2k_2t_2\bigl[\tfrac{t_2}{2}(F_2 - m_2g) + m_2v_1\bigr]}}{k_2}
\end{equation}
Substituting~\eqref{ch4:eq:48} into~\eqref{ch4:eq:46} gives the second-stage burnout velocity as
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:49}
v_2 = \frac{I_{t2} - m_2gt_2 + m_2v_1}{\sqrt{m_2^{2} + 2k_2t_2\bigl[\tfrac{t_2}{2}(F_2 - m_2g)
  + m_2v_1\bigr]}}
\end{equation}
As a check on~\eqref{ch4:eq:48} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:49}, suppose we let $y_1$ and $v_1$ both be zero;
we should then get back equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:27} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:28} for a single-staged
rocket with $k = k_2$, $F = F_2$, $t_b = t_2$, and $m = m_2$. It is readily seen that setting $y_1$
and $v_1$ to zero does indeed reduce the above equations to a form identical to~\eqref{ch4:eq:27}
and~\eqref{ch4:eq:28}.

The generalization of the burnout velocity and altitude increment equations to rockets of three or
more stages is now fairly simple. In the case of a three-staged rocket, for example, the
first-stage burnout velocity and altitude are calculated using the methods of
Section~\ref{ch4:sec:2.1}; the second-stage burnout velocity and the altitude increment gained
during the second-stage burn are then calculated by~\eqref{ch4:eq:48} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:49}, and the
third-stage altitude increment and burnout velocity are found using the same method by
which~\eqref{ch4:eq:48} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:49} were derived. In more general terms, the burnout
velocity of the $n$th stage of a multistaged rocket is given by
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:50}
v_n = \frac{I_{tn} - m_ngt_n + m_nv_{n-1}}{\sqrt{m_n^{2} + 2k_nt_n\bigl[\tfrac{t_n}{2}(F_n - m_ng)
  + m_nv_{n-1}\bigr]}}
\end{equation}
and the altitude \emph{increment} gained between burnout of the $(n-1)$th stage and the $n$th-stage
burnout is
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:51}
y_n = \frac{-m_n + \sqrt{m_n^{2} + 2k_nt_n\bigl[\tfrac{t_n}{2}(F_n - m_ng) + m_nv_{n-1}\bigr]}}{k_n}
\end{equation}
where $F_n$ and $t_n$ are the average thrust and burning time of the $n$th-stage engine, $k_n$ is
the drag parameter of the $n$th stage plus all subsequent stages, and $m_n$ is the average mass of
the $n$th stage with all subsequent stages carried atop it.

I have presented equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:50} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:51}, which are applicable to a model
rocket of any number of stages, in the interest of analytical completeness and generality. The
reader should bear in mind, however, that the construction of rockets having more than three stages
is a risky and ill-advised undertaking. The probability that all stages will fire successfully
decreases alarmingly as the number of stages increases. Multistaged model rockets, moreover, have
been observed to suffer more from ``weathercocking''---the tendency of the model, when launched, to
fly directly into the wind---than single-staged vehicles. Weathercocking, plus transient
disturbances due to the staging process itself, has even been known to cause the upper stages of
multistaged model rockets to fire in a nose-down attitude! For these reasons, the United States
Model Rocket Sporting Code of the National Association of Rocketry prohibits the launching of any
model rocket having more than three operable stages in NAR-sanctioned competition.

\subsubsection{Extended Fehskens-Malewicki Solution}\label{ch4:sec:2.2.2}

The Fehskens-Malewicki technique can be extended to multistaged model rockets by appropriately
altering the limits between which the integral~\eqref{ch4:eq:19b} is evaluated. Using the same
notation as in Section~\ref{ch4:sec:2.2.1} for a two-staged model, one obtains the integral in the
form\ednote{Equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:52} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:53} are rewritten per the Summary, with
the time $\hat{t}$ elapsed since second-stage ignition as the variable of integration. The 1973
equations read (52) $\int_{v_1}^{v_2} m_2\,dv/(F_2 - m_2g - k_2v^{2}) = \int_{t_1}^{t_1+t_2} dt$ and
(53) $\bigl[m_2/\sqrt{k_2(F_2 - m_2g)}\,\bigr]\tanh^{-1}\bigl[v\sqrt{k_2/(F_2 - m_2g)}\,\bigr]_{v_1}^{v_2}$,
an expression without a right-hand side.}
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:52}
\int_{v_1}^{v_2}\frac{m_2\,dv}{F_2 - m_2g - k_2v^{2}} = \int_{0}^{t_2} d\hat{t}
\end{equation}
Performing the indicated integration, we have
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:53}
\frac{m_2}{\sqrt{k_2(F_2 - m_2g)}}\tanh^{-1}\biggl[v\sqrt{\frac{k_2}{F_2 - m_2g}}\,\biggr]_{v_1}^{v_2}
  = t_2
\end{equation}
which, when expanded, yields
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:54}
\tanh^{-1}\biggl(v_2\sqrt{\frac{k_2}{F_2 - m_2g}}\biggr)
  - \tanh^{-1}\biggl(v_1\sqrt{\frac{k_2}{F_2 - m_2g}}\biggr) = \frac{t_2}{m_2}\sqrt{k_2(F_2 - m_2g)}
\end{equation}
Solving equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:54} for $v_2$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:55}
v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{F_2 - m_2g}{k_2}}\,\tanh\biggl[\tfrac{t_2}{m_2}\sqrt{k_2(F_2 - m_2g)}
  + \tanh^{-1}\biggl(v_1\sqrt{\frac{k_2}{F_2 - m_2g}}\biggr)\biggr]
\end{equation}

Now the upper limit of integration on the left of~\eqref{ch4:eq:52} could have been taken as $v$,
the velocity of the second stage at any time $\hat{t}$ during its burn, while the upper limit on the
right of~\eqref{ch4:eq:52} could accordingly have been taken as $\hat{t}$, where it is understood
that $\hat{t}$ is a value of time that is greater than or equal to 0 and less than or equal to
$t_2$.\ednote{Changed per the Summary, which asks for the discussion of the upper limit $t$ to be
changed to one of the upper limit $\hat{t}$, ``where $\hat{t}$ is greater than or equal to 0 and less
than or equal to $t_2$''. The 1973 sentence read ``Now the upper limit of integration on the left
of~(52) could have been taken as $v$, the velocity of the second stage at any time $t$ during its
burn, while the upper limit on the right of~(52) could accordingly have been taken as $t$, where it
is understood that $t$ is a value of time that is greater than or equal to $t_1$ and less than or
equal to $t_1 + t_2$.'' Equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:56}, which the Summary does not change, keeps
$t - t_1$ (that is, $\hat{t}$), and the sentence introducing~\eqref{ch4:eq:57} keeps the limits
$t = t_1$ and $t = t_1 + t_2$.} The
resulting expression for $v$ is entirely analogous to~\eqref{ch4:eq:55}, but gives
the second-stage velocity as a function of time:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:56}
v = \sqrt{\frac{F_2 - m_2g}{k_2}}\,\tanh\biggl[\tfrac{t - t_1}{m_2}\sqrt{k_2(F_2 - m_2g)}
  + \tanh^{-1}\biggl(v_1\sqrt{\frac{k_2}{F_2 - m_2g}}\biggr)\biggr]
\end{equation}
The altitude increment gained during the second-stage burn can then be determined by
integrating~\eqref{ch4:eq:56} from $t = t_1$ to $t = t_1 + t_2$:
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:57}
y_2 = \frac{m_2}{k_2}\ln\left[\frac{\cosh\Bigl[\tfrac{t_2}{m_2}\sqrt{k_2(F_2 - m_2g)}
  + \tanh^{-1}\Bigl(v_1\sqrt{\frac{k_2}{F_2 - m_2g}}\Bigr)\Bigr]}{\cosh\Bigl[\tanh^{-1}\Bigl(v_1
  \sqrt{\frac{k_2}{F_2 - m_2g}}\Bigr)\Bigr]}\right]
\end{equation}
and through the use of the well-known identity
\begin{equation*}
\cosh(A + B) = \cosh(A)\cosh(B) + \sinh(A)\sinh(B)
\end{equation*}
where $A$ and $B$ represent any two quantities, \eqref{ch4:eq:57} may be cast into the simpler form
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:58}
\begin{split}
y_2 = {}&\frac{m_2}{k_2}\ln\biggl[\cosh\Bigl(\tfrac{t_2}{m_2}\sqrt{k_2(F_2 - m_2g)}\Bigr)\\
&+ v_1\sqrt{\frac{k_2}{F_2 - m_2g}}\,\sinh\Bigl(\tfrac{t_2}{m_2}\sqrt{k_2(F_2 - m_2g)}\Bigr)\biggr]
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The burnout velocity and altitude increment equations can be generalized to any number of stages,
as was done in the case of the Caporaso-Bengen solution. It will be found that the burnout velocity
of the $n$th stage of a multistaged model rocket (carrying all subsequent stages atop it) is given
by
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:59}
v_n = \sqrt{\frac{F_n - m_ng}{k_n}}\,\tanh\biggl[\tfrac{t_n}{m_n}\sqrt{k_n(F_n - m_ng)}
  + \tanh^{-1}\biggl(v_{n-1}\sqrt{\frac{k_n}{F_n - m_ng}}\biggr)\biggr]
\end{equation}
while the altitude increment gained between the burnout of the $(n-1)$th stage and the burnout of
the $n$th stage is
\begin{equation}\label{ch4:eq:60}
\begin{split}
y_n = {}&\frac{m_n}{k_n}\ln\biggl[\cosh\Bigl(\tfrac{t_n}{m_n}\sqrt{k_n(F_n - m_ng)}\Bigr)\\
&+ v_{n-1}\sqrt{\frac{k_n}{F_n - m_ng}}\,\sinh\Bigl(\tfrac{t_n}{m_n}\sqrt{k_n(F_n - m_ng)}\Bigr)
  \biggr]
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The equations obtained through the application of the Caporaso-Riccati method are, as in the case
of single-staged rockets, identical to those derived by the Fehskens-Malewicki method. Consequently,
I shall not present their step-by-step development here.

The qualitative features of the multistage solutions developed in this section are all similar to
those of the corresponding single-stage solutions. For instance, it can be seen from
equations~\eqref{ch4:eq:49} and~\eqref{ch4:eq:55} that, as the burn time becomes very long, the
burnout velocity tends toward the \emph{terminal velocity} of the stage; i.e., toward that velocity
at which drag and weight exactly counterbalance thrust and no further increase in velocity is
possible. At the same time, the $t_2^{2}$ term dominates the radicand in equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:48}
and the argument of the natural logarithm function in~\eqref{ch4:eq:58} approaches a pure
exponential, so that the resulting burnout altitude increment becomes almost directly proportional
to burn time---corresponding to the model's having reached a constant (terminal) velocity. Upon
inspection, both the single-stage solutions and the multistage solutions for rockets of three or
more stages will also be found to exhibit this property. As in the case of a single-staged rocket,
the terminal velocity of an upper stage with all subsequent stages atop it depends upon the
relative magnitudes of the thrust, weight, and drag parameter. A curious consequence of this fact
is that \emph{the burnout velocity of an upper stage can be less than the first-stage burnout
velocity}. This can happen, for instance, in a two-staged rocket where a high-thrust engine is used
for the first stage and a low-thrust engine for the second. Under these conditions the burnout
velocity of the upper stage approaches the terminal velocity \emph{from above} rather than from
below. In the mid-1960's a number of model rockets were constructed for the purpose of ``breaking
the sound barrier''; that is, exceeding the 340-meter-per-second velocity of sound in sea-level
air. Since most of these vehicles relied on low-thrust sustainer stages atop high-thrust boosters,
almost all of them failed in their objective: the sustainer stage actually \emph{slowed down} after
burnout of the booster stages!

Equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:60} completes the analysis of this section, which has been directed toward
the determination of the \emph{burnout velocity} and \emph{burning-phase altitude increment} of the
upper stages of multistaged model rockets in non-oscillating, purely vertical flight. Again, for the
benefit of readers not mathematically inclined, I shall conclude with a summary of explicit
instructions for applying the results obtained.

To use the extended \emph{Caporaso-Bengen} method for a two-staged model, calculate $v_2$ using
equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:49} and $y_2$ using equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:48}. For the $n$th stage of a
multistaged rocket use equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:50} for $v_n$, \eqref{ch4:eq:51} for $y_n$.

To use the extended \emph{Fehskens-Malewicki} method for a two-staged rocket, compute $v_2$ by
equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:55} and $y_2$ by equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:58}. In analyzing the $n$th stage of
a multistaged rocket, use equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:59} for $v_n$ and equation~\eqref{ch4:eq:60} for
$y_n$.
