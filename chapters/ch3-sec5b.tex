\subsubsection{Fin-Body Interference Drag at Angle of Attack}\label{ch3:sec:5.2.3}

The subject of interference drag at angle of attack is prohibitively difficult to handle
theoretically. Consequently, we shall rely entirely upon semiempirical determinations here.
The term ``interference drag'' refers to the increment in drag a complete configuration possesses
over the sum of the drags of its separated, component parts. At the fin-body joints there is a
joining and thickening of boundary layers, leading to increased drag in this region. Separation is
a distinct possibility at such joints, and Stine~(\ref{ch3:ref:18}) suggests the elimination of
sharp corners by glue fillets to minimize this danger. Hoerner~(\ref{ch3:ref:9}) has reported data
indicating that interference drag is minimized when the fillet radius is between 4\% and 8\% of
the fin chord at the root.

Reference~(\ref{ch3:ref:6}) gives the following semiempirical expression for interference drag
coefficient at angle of attack:
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:146}
\Delta C_{Di} = \left[K_{F(B)} + K_{B(F)} - 1\right]\frac{dC_L}{d\alpha}\,\frac{S_e}{S_m}\,\alpha^2
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}[nosep,itemsep=1ex,leftmargin=*,label={}]
  \item $K_{F(B)} = \dfrac{\text{fin lift in the presence of the body}}{\text{fin lift alone}}$;
  \item $K_{B(F)} = \dfrac{\text{body lift in the presence of the fins}}{\text{fin lift alone}}$;
  \item $\dfrac{dC_L}{d\alpha}$ is the lift-curve slope of the fin at $\alpha = 0$; and
  \item $S_e$ is the exposed fin planform area.
\end{itemize}
The functions $K_{F(B)}$ and $K_{B(F)}$ are both given in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:40}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth,height=0.85\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/ch3/fig40.png}
  \caption{Fin-body interference coefficients for flight at nonzero angle of attack. $d$ is the
    diameter of the body tube, while $b$ is the span of a diametrically opposed pair of fins.}
  \label{ch3:fig:40}
\end{figure}

We can now determine $\Delta C_{Di}$ for our trial rocket. The total fin span $b$ is 7.14 cm.\ and
the body diameter $d$ is 2.06 cm., so $d/b = .289$. This gives, from Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:40},
$K_{B(F)} = 0.44$ and $K_{F(B)} = 1.25$. Since only two fins are assumed to be producing lift,
$S_e = 12.95$ cm.$^{2}$, and
\begin{equation*}
S_m = \pi r^2 = 3.33\un{cm.^{2}}
\qquad\qquad\qquad
\frac{dC_L}{d\alpha} = \frac{1}{.32} = 3.12
\end{equation*}
so that
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:147}
\Delta C_{Di} = (.44 + 1.25 - 1)\,3.12\,\frac{12.95}{3.33}\,\alpha^2 = 8.38\alpha^2
\end{equation}
The interference drag coefficient due to angle of attack is thus of considerable magnitude; in
fact, it is larger than the \emph{sum} of $C_{D_B}(\alpha)$ and $C_{Di}'$, as given by
equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:144} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:145}.\ednote{The text says that
$\Delta C_{Di}$ is larger than the \emph{sum} of $C_{D_B}(\alpha)$ and $C_{Di}'$. Over the
range of Table~\ref{ch3:tab:4} it is larger than either of them, but not than their sum:
equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:144} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:145} add up to
$8.45\alpha^{2} + 8.86\alpha^{3}$, which exceeds the $8.38\alpha^{2}$ of
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:147} at every angle of attack (at $10\dg$ the table gives
$.106 + .198 = .304$ against $.255$); only the $\alpha^{2}$ coefficients, 8.45 and 8.38, are
nearly equal. Neither the errata nor the
supplements correct this; the sentence is kept as printed.}

\subsubsection{Total Drag Increase at Angle of Attack}\label{ch3:sec:5.2.4}

If we now combine the results of~\ref{ch3:sec:5.2.1}, \ref{ch3:sec:5.2.2}, and~\ref{ch3:sec:5.2.3},
we obtain an expression for the total drag coefficient increment $\CD(\alpha)$ due to angle of
attack:
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:148}
\CD(\alpha) = C_{D_B}(\alpha) + C_{Di}' + \Delta C_{Di}
\end{equation}
For our trial rocket, the result is
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:149}
\CD(\alpha) = 16.83\alpha^2 + 8.9\alpha^3
\end{equation}
This function, along with its constituent functions, is tabulated in Table~\ref{ch3:tab:4} and is
plotted in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:41}. In order to compare equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:149} with the
experimental data for G. Harry Stine's Aerobee-Hi, the trial rocket has been assumed to have the
same zero-lift drag coefficient as the Aerobee-Hi (about 0.75). The trial vehicle would probably
have a much lower zero-lift drag coefficient in actuality (unless its surface were very rough),
but the assumption of equal zero-lift drag for the two vehicles makes it more convenient to compare
their drag increments due to angle of attack. The agreement between the curves for the two
vehicles is surprisingly close, considering the physical differences between the rockets and the
simplifying assumptions made in the derivation of equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:149}---a result which
tends to confirm the validity of the semiempirical approach. It is, however, hoped that
experiments will be performed in the near future on rockets similar to that of
Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:37} (with and without launch lug), to determine with greater precision the
accuracy of this computational method.

One fact is clearly established in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:41}: that the increase in drag at small
angles of attack can be a sizeable portion of the zero-lift drag. For the Aerobee-Hi model, the
drag coefficient is doubled at an incidence of ten degrees. Clearly, then, for maximum altitude
performance, it is desirable to design a rocket such that all oscillations resulting from
in-flight disturbances are damped out as quickly as possible.

We observe that equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:149} is dominated by the $\alpha^2$ term over the entire
range of angles of attack of interest in model rocketry; up to $\alpha = 5\dg$, the $\alpha^3$ term
may be neglected with less than 5\% error in the final result. With respect to determining the
coefficient $\epsilon$ introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch1} and used in Chapter~\ref{ch4} to compute the
effect of dynamic oscillations on altitude capability, however, the modeler will generally want
to be conservative. A conservative determination of $\epsilon$ may be made by finding the value of
$\alpha$ at which $\CD$ is doubled over its zero-lift value, according to
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:148}, and then determining the value of $\epsilon$ in the approximate
function
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:150}
\CD(\alpha) \cong \frac{2\epsilon}{\rho S_m}\,\alpha^2
\end{equation}
such that the drag is again doubled at this same value of $\alpha$. In the case of our trial
rocket, the drag coefficient will be doubled over its zero lift value when $\CD(\alpha) = 0.75$.
This occurs for $\alpha = 0.201$ radian, or about $11.5\dg$. From equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:150} we
find that the value of $\frac{2\epsilon}{\rho S_m}$ required to produce a $\CD(\alpha)$ of 0.75 at
$\alpha = 0.201$ radian is 18.52, and this would determine the value of $\epsilon$ used in
determining oscillation effects on altitude performance by the methods presented in
Chapter~\ref{ch4}. Equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:150} is conservative (that is, it slightly overestimates
the drag) over the range of angles of attack between zero and that at which the drag is
doubled---and for the average model rocket, the $\alpha$ at which $\CD$ is doubled marks the upper
limit of the range of angles of attack of interest.

\subsection{Drag Due to Rotation}\label{ch3:sec:5.3}

A model rocket, rotating about its longitudinal---or \emph{roll}---axis in flight, will experience
an increase in drag due to the thickening of the boundary layer resulting from the circumferential
velocity of the body tube surface. We shall denote this circumferential velocity component by $u$.
This thickening of the boundary layer could conceivably cause separation on the forebody and fins,
and an increased dead-air volume aft of the base, if $u$ is sufficiently large.

The drag increase due to rotation may be estimated by examining Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:42}. The
circumferential velocity is given by
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:151}
u = \pi d n
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the diameter of the body and $n$ is the number of revolutions per second. If we
assume that the trial rocket of our numerical example (diameter $= 2.06$ cm.) has been given a roll
rate of 100 radians per second (15.9 revolutions/second) at an airspeed of 60 meters (6000 cm.) per
second, we obtain
\begin{equation*}
\frac{u}{U} = 0.0172
\end{equation*}
The effect of such small ratios of body tube circumferential velocity to vehicle
airspeed---ratios typical of model rockets---is too small to be read from the curve of
Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:42}. One may therefore conclude that, as far as the finless body is
concerned, rotation does not exert a significant influence on model rocket drag.

  \centering

  \caption{Increase in the drag coefficient of a finless projectile due to rotation (spinning)
    about the longitudinal axis. $u$ is the tangential velocity of the projectile's surface due to
    the spin; $U$ is the longitudinal free-stream velocity.}
  \label{ch3:fig:42}
\end{figure}

For $\omega_Z = 100$ radians/second at $U = 6000$ cm./sec., a fin cant angle $\theta$ of 0.05093
radian ($2.918\dg$) is required. Now the effective angle of attack of the fins is always
\emph{less} than the cant angle, since the airflow itself is ``canted'' as seen by the fins affixed
to the rotating rocket. To be more precise, the angle of attack varies with the radius from the
rocket's centerline according to
\begin{equation*}
\alpha(r) = \theta - \arctan\left(\frac{\omega_Z r}{U}\right)
\end{equation*}
For $\arctan\left(\frac{\omega_Z r}{U}\right) \le$ about $15\dg$ ($= 0.262$ radian), or
$\left(\frac{\omega_Z r}{U}\right) \le$ about 0.268,
$\arctan\left(\frac{\omega_Z r}{U}\right) \cong \frac{\omega_Z r}{U}$.
That is, the ``small angle approximation'' is valid. Then the equation for $\alpha(r)$ can be
linearized to give
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:153}
\alpha(r) = \theta - \frac{\omega_Z r}{U} \qquad \text{radian}
\end{equation}
without significant loss of accuracy.

For the simple, rectangular fins of our trial rocket, the \emph{average} effective angle of attack
can be used to determine the largest component of the induced drag, and to determine the fin-body
interference drag, due to fin cant. Remembering that the fins are assumed to extend through the
body tube for the purpose of calculating induced drag, each one of the four canted fins is assumed
to run from the rocket's centerline to the fin tip. The effective angle of attack $\alpha(r)$ at the
centerline (where $r = 0$) is just $\theta = 0.05093$ radian, while $\alpha(r)$ at the fin tip is
$-0.00857$ radian. The negative effective angle of attack near the tip creates the roll damping
moment that balances the roll forcing moment to produce the equilibrium roll rate $\omega_Z$ given
by equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:115} of Chapter~\ref{ch2} and equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:152} above. Since
$\alpha(r)$ varies linearly with radial distance from the centerline, the average effective angle
of attack $\bar{\alpha}$ is just
\begin{equation*}
\bar{\alpha} = (0.05093 - 0.00857)/2 = 0.02118\un{radian}
\end{equation*}
In equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:145} the induced drag of two diametrically opposed fins of the trial
rocket was found to be $6.51\alpha^2$. In the case of canted fins, all four fins are at the angle
of attack $\bar{\alpha}$; equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:145} must therefore be doubled to give
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:154}
(C_{Di}')_{\mathrm{cant}} = 13.02\bar{\alpha}^2
\end{equation}

The linear variation of $\alpha(r)$ with radial distance from the centerline also makes the fins
appear to the relative airstream as if they were twisted. This \emph{aerodynamic twist}, denoted
$\Delta\alpha$, gives rise to an additional component of induced drag. Although less than the
component of induced drag due to the average effective angle of attack, this additional induced
drag component can be of significant magnitude. Hoerner~(\ref{ch3:ref:9}) gives the approximate
value of the coefficient of induced drag due to twist as
\begin{equation*}
(C_{Di})_{\mathrm{twist}} = (4 \times 10^{-5})(\Delta\alpha)^2
\qquad \text{for $\Delta\alpha$ given in degrees}
\end{equation*}
For $\Delta\alpha$ given in radians, the equation becomes
\begin{equation*}
(C_{Di})_{\mathrm{twist}} = 0.1313(\Delta\alpha)^2
\end{equation*}
This coefficient is based on the fin area. To convert it to a coefficient based on the maximum body
cross-sectional area we must multiply it by $S_F/S_m$, where $S_F$ is the total area of all four
canted fins:
\begin{align*}
(C_{Di}')_{\mathrm{twist}} &= (C_{Di})_{\mathrm{twist}}\,\frac{4\,(3.57 \times 2.54)}{\pi(1.03)^2}\\
&= 10.8828\,(C_{Di})_{\mathrm{twist}}\\
(C_{Di}')_{\mathrm{twist}} &= 1.43(\Delta\alpha)^2 \tag{155$'$}\label{ch3:eq:n155}
\end{align*}

From equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:147}, we obtain the fin-body interference drag component due to the 4
canted fins as
\begin{align*}
(\Delta C_{Di})_{\mathrm{cant}} &= 2(8.38\bar{\alpha}^2)\\
&= 16.76\bar{\alpha}^2
\end{align*}

The total drag increase due to fin cant is then
\begin{equation*}\tag{156$'$}\label{ch3:eq:n156}
(\Delta\CD)_{\mathrm{cant}} = (C_{Di}')_{\mathrm{cant}} + (C_{Di}')_{\mathrm{twist}}
+ (\Delta C_{Di})_{\mathrm{cant}}
\end{equation*}
which, for our trial rocket, becomes
\begin{equation*}
(\Delta\CD)_{\mathrm{cant}} = (13.02 + 16.76)\bar{\alpha}^2 + 1.43(\Delta\alpha)^2
\end{equation*}
The average effective angle of attack $\bar{\alpha}$ has been calculated as 0.02118 radian. The
total aerodynamic twist $\Delta\alpha$ is the difference between the effective angle of attack at
the centerline and the effective angle of attack at the fin tip:
\begin{align*}
\Delta\alpha &= 0.05093 - (-0.00857)\\
&= 0.0595\un{radian}
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{align*}
(\Delta\CD)_{\mathrm{cant}} &= (13.02 + 16.76)(0.02118)^2 + 1.43(0.0595)^2\\
&= 0.018433
\end{align*}
If the rocket's zero-incidence drag coefficient is 0.75 without fin cant, this means a 2.46\%
increase in $\CDo$. If a spin rate double that used in the above calculations were desired, it
would be necessary to incur four times this drag penalty, or an increase of 9.84\% in $\CDo$. If
triple the example spin rate were desired, it would be necessary to incur nine times the example's
drag penalty, or an increase of 22.14\% in $\CDo$. These figures indicate that, while the effects
of rotation themselves may not be significant, the effects of the mechanism by which rotation is
induced may be considerable. Furthermore, there is an increasing danger of separation in the vicinity
of the fin leading edges as the angle of cant is increased. Such separation could result in a
gross magnification of pressure drag. While the angle of cant at which such phenomena become
significant has not as yet been established with precision, it has been generally accepted for a
number of years that cant angles greater than $15\dg$ produce reductions in performance so severe
as to be detectable with the naked eye.

\subsection{Drag Due to Surface Roughness in Turbulent Flow}\label{ch3:sec:5.4}

In Section~\ref{ch3:sec:3.5} we examined the critical height of roughness elements necessary to
induce premature transition in the boundary layer from laminar to turbulent flow. Within the
turbulent boundary layer which is prevalent over most of a model rocket at higher Reynolds numbers,
roughness can affect the drag in another manner: if the individual roughness particles protrude
above the thin laminar sublayer, they will have a viscous drag of their own. The turbulent
skin-friction coefficient derived in Section~\ref{ch3:sec:3} (equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:86}) applies
only to ``hydraulically smooth'' surfaces; that is, surfaces on which the grain size $k$ of
roughness particles is less than the thickness of the laminar sublayer.

The \emph{admissible height} $k_{\mathrm{adm}}$ for roughness particles is defined as the maximum
height of the particles which gives no increase in the drag compared with a smooth wall. A simple,
conservative formula for determining $k_{\mathrm{adm}}$ for a flat plate is~(\ref{ch3:ref:15}):
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:155}
k_{\mathrm{adm}} \le 100\,\frac{\nu}{U_\infty}
\end{equation}
This relationship is accurate for Reynolds numbers below about $1 \times 10^{6}$. It does not take
into account the fact that the boundary-layer thickness increases with distance from the leading
edge, and hence that $k_{\mathrm{adm}}$ is smaller upstream than downstream. Its use is justified,
however, because it provides values of $k_{\mathrm{adm}}$ which are generally smaller than those
obtained from the more precise expression~(\ref{ch3:ref:15})
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:156}
k_{\mathrm{adm}} < \frac{7\nu}{U_\infty\sqrt{C_{fx}}}
\end{equation}
where $C_{fx}$ is the local skin-friction coefficient, given by
$\frac{2\tau_o}{\rho U_\infty^2}$. Equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:155} may thus be used for the entire
range of model rocket Reynolds numbers without fear of obtaining values of $k_{\mathrm{adm}}$ which
are too large.

If we let $U = 60$ meters/second, for instance, and $\nu = 1.495 \times 10^{-5}$
meter$^{2}$/second, equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:155} yields
\begin{equation*}
k_{\mathrm{adm}} \le 2.48 \times 10^{-5}\un{meter} = 2.48 \times 10^{-3}\un{cm.}
\end{equation*}
This result applies specifically to sand grains, for which equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:155} was
empirically determined, but it can be used as an approximate guide for other forms of roughness.
Hence, at a velocity typical of model rocket flight, the surface of the model downstream of the
transition point may be regarded as hydraulically smooth if the size of distributed roughness
grains does not exceed about 0.0025 cm. If this condition is satisfied, the smooth turbulent
skin-friction coefficient of Section~\ref{ch3:sec:3} may be used in calculations. It should be
noted that the calculated value of $k_{\mathrm{adm}}$ is considerably less than the roughness height
required to induce premature transition as determined in Section~\ref{ch3:sec:3.5}. If the
criterion of this section is satisfied, therefore, transition will not be induced prematurely.

Table~\ref{ch3:tab:5} provides information on grain sizes in microns (1 micron
$= 1 \times 10^{-6}$ meter $= 1 \times 10^{-4}$ cm.) for various surfaces. It indicates that the
finish on a model rocket should be about as smooth as that of paint on mass-produced aircraft, or
better. Note that \emph{poorly-sprayed} paint has a grain size of about 200 microns---\emph{eight
times} our calculated $k_{\mathrm{adm}}$. A rocket with such a poor finish will have a considerably
higher skin-friction coefficient than one that is sufficiently well painted to be hydraulically
smooth. The skin-friction coefficient of a surface having roughness elements sufficiently large
that it cannot be considered hydraulically smooth may be found from the semiempirical
formula~(\ref{ch3:ref:15})
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:157}
C_f = \left(1.89 + 1.62\log\frac{\ell_b}{k}\right)^{-2.5}
\end{equation}
which is valid for values of $\frac{\ell_b}{k}$ between $10^{2}$ and $10^{6}$, where $\ell_b$
denotes the length of the body (or fin, as the case may be), and the notation ``log'' denotes the
logarithm to the base 10. According to equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:157}, a body 30 cm.\ in length
having roughness particles of $k = 0.02$ cm.\ will exhibit a friction drag coefficient of
\begin{equation*}
C_f = 7.63 \times 10^{-3}
\end{equation*}
The corresponding value for a hydraulically smooth body in turbulent flow is $4.5 \times 10^{-3}$,
so in this instance roughness increases the friction drag by about 70\%.

Equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:157} applies only to surfaces which are completely rough; i.e., those for
which the height of the roughness elements is everywhere greater than the thickness of the laminar
sublayer. The degree of roughness of a given surface thus depends upon $k/\delta$, the ratio of
grain size to boundary-layer thickness. Since $\delta$ varies with Reynolds number, a given surface
may appear rough or smooth, depending on the value of the free-stream velocity. If, for example,
we examine a turbulent boundary layer over a surface at relatively low Reynolds numbers (around
$5 \times 10^{5}$), the roughness elements may be completely submerged in the laminar sublayer,
making the surface appear hydraulically smooth. As the velocity (and therefore the Reynolds number)
increases, the boundary layer becomes thinner, and the roughness elements begin to protrude from
the sublayer. This process is initiated near the nose, or leading edge, where the boundary layer is
thinnest, and progresses downstream with increasing $R$. When a surface is in this ``transition''
state between a completely smooth and a completely rough appearance, its skin-friction coefficient
will have a value intermediate between those determined from equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:86}
and~\eqref{ch3:eq:157}. Note that this process proceeds along a body in a direction opposite to
that of laminar-to-turbulent boundary-layer transition, as described in
Section~\ref{ch3:sec:3.5.1}.

Two final observations can be made regarding drag due to roughness. First, since
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:157} is independent of velocity, $C_f$ is a constant for any given ratio
$\frac{\ell_b}{k}$. In accordance with equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:18}, it may then be concluded that
hydraulically rough surfaces obey the quadratic drag law exactly. Furthermore,
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:155} for $k_{\mathrm{adm}}$ is independent of body length; hence \emph{any
two} rockets travelling at the same velocity under identical atmospheric conditions have identical
values of $k_{\mathrm{adm}}$. The maximum velocity attained by a rocket determines the minimum
value of $k_{\mathrm{adm}}$. If this minimum grain size is not exceeded anywhere on the surface, the
rocket may be regarded as hydraulically smooth throughout its flight, and the friction drag
equations of Section~\ref{ch3:sec:3} are applicable to the calculation of its drag coefficient.
