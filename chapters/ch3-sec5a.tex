\section{Other Contributions to Model Rocket Drag}\label{ch3:sec:5}

\subsection{Introduction}\label{ch3:sec:5.1}

The two preceding sections examined the contributions to drag of the tangential
(viscous) and normal (pressure) forces acting on a model rocket body. Several
important assumptions were inherent in these presentations; namely that (a) the
body was inclined at zero incidence (zero angle of attack) to the flow; (b) the
body was not rotating about its longitudinal axis; and (c) surface roughness
affected the drag only through inducement of premature transition. We are now
interested in determining the effects on the drag of relaxing these
assumptions.

The determination of tail-body drag at angle of attack is extremely difficult,
because little is known about tail-body interference effects at different flow
inclinations. We will restrict our attention to small angles of attack (less
than about $10\dg$), since at larger values of $\alpha$ the relationship between
drag and angle of attack is far more complex and, in any case, flight at large
angles of attack generally indicates a dynamic stability problem necessitating
a redesign of the rocket. Furthermore, only very simple single-stage model
rocket configurations will be examined. Article~\ref{ch3:sec:5.2} presents a
practical method (from Reference~\ref{ch3:ref:6}) for calculating the drag
coefficient at angles of attack, although (as with many methods discussed in
this chapter) it still requires experimental data to confirm its applicability
to model rockets.

In Section~\ref{ch3:sec:5.3} we examine the effects of rotation about the roll
axis, which generally produces a drag increase. It will be found that the
increase in drag coefficient due to spin is usually small compared to that due
to the mechanism which induces the rotation (usually canted fins).

Previously, in Section~\ref{ch3:sec:3.5.2}, it was seen that surface roughness
due to single or distributed particles can lead to premature transition, and
hence to an increase in the skin friction. In a purely turbulent region surface
imperfections, since they represent obstacles to the flow, have a viscous drag
of their own. This component of model rocket drag will be examined in
Section~\ref{ch3:sec:5.4}.

\subsection{Drag at Small Angles of Attack}\label{ch3:sec:5.2}

Due to forces which may arise from causes such as wind gusts, off-center
thrust, misaligned fins, or staging transients, a model rocket may assume an
angle of attack $\alpha$ to the instantaneous velocity vector, as seen in
Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:37}. If the vehicle is aerodynamically stable the angle is
usually quite small and quickly reduced by the restoring moment and damping
moment characteristic of a stable model. During the time in which the response
to the disturbance produces discernable angles of attack, however, the drag may
be increased considerably. A knowledge of the extent to which the drag
coefficient of a given rocket increases with angle of attack is therefore of
considerable value. In Sections~\ref{ch3:sec:5.2.1} and~\ref{ch3:sec:5.2.2}
expressions will be presented for estimating the increase in drag coefficient
of the body and tailfin assembly alone, respectively, with angle of attack. The
magnitude of the corrections required due to fin-body interference will be
discussed in~\ref{ch3:sec:5.2.3}, and the overall variation of $\CD$ with angle
of attack, as found by experiment and through semiempirical formulae, is
analyzed in~\ref{ch3:sec:5.2.4}.

\subsubsection{Body Drag at Angle of Attack}\label{ch3:sec:5.2.1}

The drag of a slender body (such as that of a model rocket) at an angle of
attack is closely related to its side force (if any) and drag at zero angle of
attack. The total drag coefficient of a model rocket body (nosecone plus
cylindrical body tube, but excluding fins, launch lugs, and any other
protuberances) at a nonzero angle of attack can be expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:136}
C_{D_B} = (\CDo)_B + C_{D_B}(\alpha)
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,label={}]
  \item $(\CDo)_B$ is the body drag coefficient at zero angle of attack. A
  detailed method of estimating this quantity is presented in
  Section~\ref{ch3:sec:6}. A typical value for a slender model rocket is
  $(\CDo)_B = 0.27$ at a Reynolds number of $1 \times 10^{6}$. This coefficient
  includes base drag.
  \item $C_{D_B}(\alpha)$ is the drag coefficient of the body due to angle of
  attack (the notation is read ``$C_{D_B}$ as a function of $\alpha$'', or
  simply ``$C_{D_B}$ of $\alpha$''), which will be discussed herein.
\end{itemize}

When a model rocket assumes a very small angle of attack (about 2 degrees or
less), the external flow is not significantly disturbed from its behavior at
zero incidence. Since it was shown in Section~\ref{ch3:sec:4} that (for a
streamlined nose) this external flow resembles closely that predicted for an
inviscid fluid, it is reasonable to attempt the use of potential flow theory for
the determination of aerodynamic forces at very small angles of attack---and,
indeed, such analyses have been carried out. The forces produced are a side
force, perpendicular to the free-stream flow direction, and a much smaller drag
force, parallel to $U_\infty$.

Potential theory predicts a side force (also referred to as ``lift'')
coefficient which varies linearly with angle of attack. According to Van
Dyke~(\ref{ch3:ref:6}), the body lift-curve slope at zero incidence is given by
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:137}
\frac{dC_L}{d\alpha} = \frac{2(k_2 - k_1)}{S_m}S_o
\end{equation}
so that the lift coefficient $C_L$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:138}
C_L = \frac{2(k_2 - k_1)S_o}{S_m}\alpha
\end{equation}
Applying the approximation $\sin(\alpha) \cong \alpha$, valid for small angles,
we then have\ednote{The last member of equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:139} is printed
with the factor $\alpha$. The factor $\alpha^{2}$ is evidently meant: it is
what $\alpha C_L$ gives with $C_L$ from equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:138}, and the
first term of equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:142} and the numerical example below, which
gives $1.94\alpha^{2}$, have it. Neither the errata nor the supplements correct
this; the equation is kept as printed.}
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:139}
C_{D_B}(\alpha) = \alpha C_L = \frac{2(k_2 - k_1)S_o}{S_m}\alpha
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,label={}]
  \item $(k_2 - k_1)$ is the ``apparent mass factor'' as determined by Munk,
  given in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:35} as a function of body fineness ratio.
  \item $x$ is the distance from the nosecone tip measured along the
  longitudinal body axis.
  \item $x_o$ is the body station where the flow ceases to obey the predictions
  of potential theory. This location can be found from the expression
  \begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:140}
  x_o = 0.55x_1 + 0.36\ell_b
  \end{equation}
  \item $x_1$ is the body station where the rate of change of cross-sectional
  area with $x$ $(dS_x/dx)$ first reaches its minimum value.
  \item $S_x$ is the body cross-sectional area at station $x$.
  \item $S_o$ is the body cross-sectional area at $x_o$.
  \item $S_m$ is the maximum body cross-sectional area.
\end{itemize}
These terms will be clarified when a numerical example is computed shortly. It
should be noted that equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:140} has been constructed by slope
and intercept extrapolation from a graph contained in
Reference~\ref{ch3:ref:6}, and that the validity of this extrapolation has not
as yet been demonstrated.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=0.85\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/ch3/fig35.png}
  \caption{Apparent mass factor $(k_2 - k_1)$ as a function of body fineness
  ratio $(\ell_b/d)$.}
  \label{ch3:fig:35}
\end{figure}

As the angle of attack increases beyond about two degrees, separation of the
boundary layer from the leeward side of the body occurs and potential theory no
longer yields an accurate result; the \emph{normal} (i.e., perpendicular)
forces, and hence the lift, increase nonlinearly with angle of attack. The flow
in the boundary layer exhibits a component in the circumferential direction due
to the increasing magnitude of \emph{viscous cross-flow forces}. Theories have
been developed which take account of these effects by adding to the
potential-flow solution of equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:138} and~\eqref{ch3:eq:139}
another, viscous term. The method we will present here assumes, as before, that
the flow is potential over the forward part of the body and that there is no
viscous contribution to drag due solely to the angle of attack. On the aft part
of the body, where the flow is assumed entirely viscous, lift and drag arise
solely from cross-flow forces. Then, according to Hopkins~(\ref{ch3:ref:6}),
\begin{align}
C_L &= \frac{2(k_2 - k_1)S_o}{S_m}\alpha
  + \frac{2\alpha^2}{S_m}\int_{x_o}^{\ell_b} \eta r_x C_{Dc}\,dx
  \label{ch3:eq:141}\\
C_{D_B}(\alpha) &= \frac{2(k_2 - k_1)S_o}{S_m}\alpha^2
  + \frac{2\alpha^3}{S_m}\int_{x_o}^{\ell_b} \eta r_x C_{Dc}\,dx
  \label{ch3:eq:142}
\end{align}
where
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,label={}]
  \item $\eta$ is the ratio of the cross-flow drag on a cylinder of
  \emph{finite} length to the cross-flow drag on a cylinder of \emph{infinite}
  length, given in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:36};
  \item $C_{Dc}$ is the experimental steady-state cross-flow drag coefficient of
  a circular cylinder of infinite length. $C_{Dc}$ is equal to 1.2 for all
  practical model rocket problems; and
  \item $r_x$ is the body radius at any station $x$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=0.85\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/ch3/fig36.png}
  \caption{Ratio of the drag coefficient of a circular cylinder of finite length
  to the drag coefficient of a circular cylinder of infinite length, $\eta$, as
  a function of cylinder fineness ratio $(\ell_b/d)$.}
  \label{ch3:fig:36}
\end{figure}

The reader is reminded here that equations~\eqref{ch3:eq:138},
\eqref{ch3:eq:139}, \eqref{ch3:eq:141}, and~\eqref{ch3:eq:142} must be applied
using $\alpha$ given in \emph{radians} rather than degrees. In most cases,
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:142} is quite simple to apply to model rockets, as $x_o$
(the station where the external flow ceases to be potential) usually occurs
downstream of the nosecone-body joint. The quantity $S_o/S_m$ thus usually
equals 1.0, and the integral, since $\eta$, $r_x$, and $C_{Dc}$ are all
constant along the aft section of a rocket having no shoulder or boattail,
involves only an exact differential $dx$. In such a case,
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:143}
\int_{x_o}^{\ell_b} \eta r_x C_{Dc}\,dx = (\ell_b - x_o)\eta r_x C_{Dc}
\end{equation}

In order to acquire some ``feel'' for the kind of numerical results one obtains
from equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:142}, we shall employ the simple, single-stage
model rocket configuration shown in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:37}. Its length, 13
inches (33 cm.)\ and body diameter, 0.813 inch (2.06 cm.), are identical to the
corresponding dimensions of the Aerobee-Hi for which Stine~(\ref{ch3:ref:18})
has reported the experimentally-determined variation of the total $\CD$ with
$\alpha$. We do not seek or expect good agreement with Stine's data, as the
methods of this and the following sections are approximate, and as the trial
rocket is somewhat different from the Aerobee-Hi. Specifically, the trial
rocket has 4 fins as opposed to 3 for the Aerobee-Hi, a different fin shape, no
launch lug, and is without the Aerobee's longitudinal fairings. Comparing the
experimental results with analytical predictions for a rocket of roughly
similar configuration, however, will give us a ``feel'' for whether our results
are, in the colloquialism of engineering, ``in the right ball park''.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=0.85\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/ch3/fig37.png}
  \caption{Trial rocket used to compute drag increase due to nonzero angle of
  attack. All dimensions are given in centimeters.}
  \label{ch3:fig:37}
\end{figure}

From Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:37}, $\ell_b/d = 16$, for which
Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:35} yields $(k_2 - k_1) = 0.97$. The quantity $dS_x/dx$
attains its minimum value, zero, at the body-nosecone junction, so
$x_1 = 8.9$ cm. We determine $x_o$ from equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:140} as follows:
\begin{equation*}
x_o = .55(8.9) + .36(33) = 16.8\un{cm.}
\end{equation*}
Since $x_o$ is located on the cylindrical body, $S_o/S_m = 1.0$; the first term
in equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:142} then becomes
\begin{equation*}
2(k_2 - k_1)\frac{S_o}{S_m}\alpha^2 = 1.94\alpha^2
\end{equation*}
From Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:36}, $\eta = 0.74$. The body radius $r_x$ is a
constant, 1.03 cm., between $x_o$ and $\ell_b$. Hence,
\begin{align*}
\frac{2\alpha^3}{S_m}\eta r_x C_{Dc}(\ell_b - x_o)
  &= \alpha^3\left[\frac{2\times .74\times 1.03\times 1.2\times 16.2}
     {\pi\times(1.03)^2}\right]\\
  &= 8.86\alpha^3
\end{align*}
Finally, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:144}
C_{D_B}(\alpha) = 1.94\alpha^2 + 8.86\alpha^3
\end{equation}

This coefficient is based on maximum body frontal area $S_m$. For
$\alpha \le 0.022$ radian (about $1.25\dg$), the $\alpha^2$ term is at least
ten times greater than the $\alpha^3$ term. This essentially defines the range
of validity for the potential-flow solution. The two terms are of equal
magnitude at $\alpha = 12.5\dg$. It should be noted here that
$C_{D_B}(\alpha)$ is independent of velocity, a result which agrees with the
predictions of slender-body theory for incompressible flow.

\subsubsection{Fin Drag at Angle of Attack}\label{ch3:sec:5.2.2}

A model rocket fin, which is here assumed to have a symmetrical, streamlined
section profile, will behave like an aircraft wing when it is inclined at an
angle to the flow. It will naturally produce a side force, or ``lift'', as
shown in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:38}---which, after all, is why rockets have fins in
the first place. Due to the deflection of the flow in the vicinity of the fin,
however, the total aerodynamic \emph{normal force} $N$ will not be
perpendicular to the free-stream flow. Instead, it is turned backwards to some
angle $\alpha_i$ to the line perpendicular to the free stream, so that it
exhibits a drag component. This drag component is known as the \emph{induced
drag} of the fin, and its associated drag coefficient is denoted $C_{Di}$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=0.85\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/ch3/fig38.png}
  \caption{The origin of induced drag. The fin is held at an angle of attack
  $\alpha$, causing the force $\vec{F}$ to act through the center of pressure.
  $\vec{F}$ is tilted back at the angle $\alpha_i$ and is resolved into lift
  $\vec{L}$ and induced drag $\vec{D}_i$.}
  \label{ch3:fig:38}
\end{figure}

As in the case of the body, the induced drag of a fin is related to the lift
force it produces. The induced drag coefficient of a fin flying at an angle of
attack $\alpha$ such that its lift coefficient is $C_L$ is given
by\ednote{From this sentence through equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:n144B} the text
and equations are those of the supplement ``Corrections and additions to
Section 5.2.2 of Chapter~3'', which replace the 1973 sentence ``One cannot,
however, obtain an expression for $C_{Di}$ unless he has specific knowledge of
the \emph{distribution} of lift on the fin.'' The same supplement adds the
constant $e = 0.863$ to the row of Hoerner's values below, which in 1973 gave
only $d\alpha\dg/dC_L = 18.6$ and $d\CD/dC_L^{2} = .123$; with $\AR = 3$ this
$e$ makes the $1/(\pi e\AR)$ of equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:n144A} equal to that
$.123$. The rest of Section~\ref{ch3:sec:5.2.2} is unchanged.}
\begin{equation*}\tag{144A}\label{ch3:eq:n144A}
C_{Di} = \frac{C_L^{2}}{\pi e\AR}
\end{equation*}
where
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,label={}]
  \item $\AR$ = aspect ratio, the span of a ``wing'' formed by a pair of
  diametrically-opposed fins divided by the average fin chord, also equal to
  $(\text{span})^{2}/(\text{area})$
\end{itemize}
and
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.5em,label={}]
  \item $e$ = span efficiency factor
\end{itemize}
The quantity $e\AR$ is the \textbf{effective aspect ratio} of an
``equivalent'' wing whose lift is distributed across its span in the shape of
an ellipse. In practice, $e$ is almost always less than 1.0. Since
\begin{equation*}
C_L = \left(\frac{dC_L}{d\alpha}\right)\alpha
\end{equation*}
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:n144A} can also be written in terms of $\alpha$ as
\begin{equation*}\tag{144B}\label{ch3:eq:n144B}
C_{Di} = \left(\frac{dC_L}{d\alpha}\right)^{2}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\pi e\AR}
\end{equation*}
We shall assume for the purposes of our numerical example that the following
values given by Hoerner~(\ref{ch3:ref:9}) for a rectangular wing of aspect ratio
$\AR = \text{span/chord} = 3$ with sharp chordwise edges are valid for the trial
rocket:
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d\alpha\dg}{dC_L} = 18.6 \qquad \frac{d\CD}{dC_L^2} = 0.123 \qquad e = 0.863
\end{equation*}
Then for $\alpha$ given in radians,
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d\alpha}{dC_L} = 0.32
\end{equation*}
so that
\begin{equation*}
C_{Di} = .123\,C_L^2 = .123\left(\frac{\alpha}{.32}\right)^2 = 1.2\alpha^2
\end{equation*}
This coefficient, based on fin planform area, can be converted to a coefficient
based on maximum body frontal area by multiplying by the factor $S_F/S_m$. We
shall assume the angle of attack to exist entirely about the $x$-axis (yaw) or
$y$-axis (pitch) of the rocket, so that only two fins are producing lift. Then
\begin{equation*}
\frac{S_F}{S_m} = \frac{7.14\times 2.54}{\pi(1.03)^2} = 5.42
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation}\label{ch3:eq:145}
C_{Di}' = 5.42\times 1.2\alpha^2 = 6.51\alpha^2
\end{equation}
You should note here that the fin planform area used includes the imaginary
extension of the fins within the body tube, and that ``planform'' refers to the
fact that only the area of \emph{one side} of the fins is used.

The assumption of square chordwise edges (squared tips) in the above
calculations is of some significance, as the shape of the fin tip can affect
the value of the induced drag considerably. When a fin is producing lift, it has
a lower pressure on its upper, or ``suction'' side, than on its lower side. This
phenomenon is associated with the formation of \emph{trailing vortices} (see
Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:39}a) near the fin tips as the air from below tries to
``curl up'' around the tips. Depending on the fin tip shape, the distance
between the resulting vortex cores may be less than the actual span of the fins,
leading to a reduction in effective span---and therefore, to a reduction in
effective aspect ratio. Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:39}b depicts several possible
fin-tip shapes, with their changes in effective aspect ratio $\Delta\AR$ and the
location of the vortex core in each case. Those shapes for which $\Delta\AR$ is
positive or zero are most desirable, since they produce low induced drag. Of
the shapes shown, this category would include numbers 1, 5, and 6. One shape
not shown, but popular among designers of high-performance model rockets, is
the \emph{elliptical} fin. During the 1930's it was shown mathematically that,
for airplane wings of moderate aspect ratio and without twist (``washin'' or
``washout'' at the tips), an elliptical planform gives the least induced drag
for a given lift. Elliptical or near-elliptical wing planforms were
subsequently incorporated into several fighter planes of the World War II era,
most famous among them being the Supermarine Spitfire of the English Royal Air
Force. It is not known for certain whether the elliptical planform retains its
advantage in the presence of a body tube of diameter commensurate with the fin
span, and in fact the testing of model rocket fin planform shapes for induced
drag is currently a pressing need which, it is hoped, advanced hobbyists will
shortly fulfil. Nevertheless, the elliptical planform is currently preferred by
a number of successful competition modelers.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=0.85\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/ch3/fig39.png}
  \caption{Trailing vortices and effective aspect ratio. An airplane wing or a
  pair of diametrically opposed rocket fins sheds trailing vortices from its
  tips (a). As viewed from behind the wing the left vortex rotates clockwise;
  the right one counterclockwise as shown. The vortices descend after leaving
  the wing and also ``tuck in'' slightly, reducing the effective span of the
  wing and therefore reducing the effective aspect ratio, since the aspect ratio
  $\AR$ is defined as the span divided by the average chord. Panel (b) shows the
  change in effective aspect ratio and vortex core position (dotted line)
  measured for a number of different tip shapes on wings of $\AR = 3.0$. Since
  wings of higher $\AR$ can generate a given lift at lower angle of attack and
  lower induced drag than wings of lower $\AR$, zero or positive values of
  $\Delta\AR$ are desirable.}
  \label{ch3:fig:39}
\end{figure}
