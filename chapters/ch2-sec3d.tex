\subsubsection{Complete Response to Step Input}\label{ch2:sec:3.2.2}

In this section I am going to derive the equations describing the angular
oscillations of a rolling model rocket subjected to a step input in yaw alone,
of the same form as that considered in Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.1.2}. This can be
done without loss of generality because the principle of superposition
(Section~\ref{ch2:sec:2.4}) permits us to obtain the solution of the effect of
each component of a step input having both pitch and yaw components separately,
and then to add the solutions thus obtained to form the complete solution. It
is also true that the response to a step in pitch is analogous to the response
to a step in yaw, so that everything a designer needs to know can be learned by
doing the yaw problem alone. For this same reason the impulse response of
Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.1.3} will be done assuming the only disturbing moment to
act about the yaw axis.

Before time equal to zero, when the value of the step disturbance is zero, the
coupled dynamical equations appear as
\begin{align*}
I_L\frac{d^{2}\alpha_X}{dt^{2}} + C_2\frac{d\alpha_X}{dt} + C_1\alpha_X
  + I_R\omega_Z\frac{d\alpha_Y}{dt} &= 0\\
I_L\frac{d^{2}\alpha_Y}{dt^{2}} + C_2\frac{d\alpha_Y}{dt} + C_1\alpha_Y
  - I_R\omega_Z\frac{d\alpha_X}{dt} &= 0
\end{align*}
As in Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.1.2}, we are considering a rocket whose pitch and
yaw states are quiescent before the application of the step. The solution to
the above equations is therefore
\begin{equation*}
\alpha_X = \alpha_Y = 0
\end{equation*}
which, as you can see, identically satisfies them both. After the step
disturbance rises to the value $M_s$ the equations become
\begin{align*}
I_L\frac{d^{2}\alpha_X}{dt^{2}} + C_2\frac{d\alpha_X}{dt} + C_1\alpha_X
  + I_R\omega_Z\frac{d\alpha_Y}{dt} &= M_s\\
I_L\frac{d^{2}\alpha_Y}{dt^{2}} + C_2\frac{d\alpha_Y}{dt} + C_1\alpha_Y
  - I_R\omega_Z\frac{d\alpha_X}{dt} &= 0
\end{align*}
The particular solution to this set of equations is known to be
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:61}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_X &= \frac{M_s}{C_1}\\
\alpha_Y &= 0
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The complete solution to the coupled step response for time greater than zero
is then
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:62}
\begin{align}
\alpha_X &= A_1 e^{-D_1 t}\sin(\omega_1 t + \varphi_1)
  + A_2 e^{-D_2 t}\sin(\omega_2 t + \varphi_2) + \frac{M_s}{C_1}
  \label{ch2:eq:62a}\\
\alpha_Y &= A_1 e^{-D_1 t}\cos(\omega_1 t + \varphi_1)
  + A_2 e^{-D_2 t}\cos(\omega_2 t + \varphi_2)
  \label{ch2:eq:62b}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are given by equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:56} and
$D_1$ and $D_2$ are given by equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:57}. $A_1$, $A_2$,
$\varphi_1$, and $\varphi_2$ are determined by the initial conditions of the
motion, which, for a quiescent state prior to the disturbance, are all zero:
\begin{align*}
\alpha_{X0} &= A_1\sin\varphi_1 + A_2\sin\varphi_2 + \frac{M_s}{C_1}\\
  &= 0\\
\alpha_{Y0} &= A_1\cos\varphi_1 + A_2\cos\varphi_2\\
  &= 0\\
\Omega_{X0} &= -A_1 D_1\sin\varphi_1 + A_1\omega_1\cos\varphi_1
  - A_2 D_2\sin\varphi_2 + A_2\omega_2\cos\varphi_2\\
  &= 0\\
\Omega_{Y0} &= -A_1 D_1\cos\varphi_1 - A_1\omega_1\sin\varphi_1
  - A_2 D_2\cos\varphi_2 - A_2\omega_2\sin\varphi_2\\
  &= 0
\end{align*}
These equations can be cast into the form of the initial condition equations
used in Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.2.1} for determining the values of the initial
amplitudes and phase angles given in equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:58}
and~\eqref{ch2:eq:59} by rewriting them in the following manner:
\begin{align*}
-\frac{M_s}{C_1} &= A_1\sin\varphi_1 + A_2\sin\varphi_2\\
0 &= A_1\cos\varphi_1 + A_2\cos\varphi_2\\
0 &= -A_1 D_1\sin\varphi_1 + A_1\omega_1\cos\varphi_1
  - A_2 D_2\sin\varphi_2 + A_2\omega_2\cos\varphi_2\\
0 &= -A_1 D_1\cos\varphi_1 - A_1\omega_1\sin\varphi_1
  - A_2 D_2\cos\varphi_2 - A_2\omega_2\sin\varphi_2
\end{align*}
By analogy with the corresponding solutions to the equations of
Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.2.1} we can then write
\begin{align*}
A_1\sin\varphi_1 &= \frac{M_s}{C_1}\left[
  \frac{(\omega_2^{2} + D_2^{2} - \omega_1\omega_2 - D_1 D_2)}
       {2(D_1 D_2 + \omega_1\omega_2) - D_1^{2} - D_2^{2} - \omega_1^{2} - \omega_2^{2}}
  \right]\\
A_1\cos\varphi_1 &= \frac{M_s}{C_1}\left[
  \frac{(\omega_1 D_2 - \omega_2 D_1)}
       {2(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2) - \omega_1^{2} - D_1^{2} - \omega_2^{2} - D_2^{2}}
  \right]
\end{align*}
from which we obtain
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:63}
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:63a}
\varphi_1 = \arctan\left[
  \frac{\omega_2^{2} + D_2^{2} - \omega_1\omega_2 - D_1 D_2}
       {\omega_1 D_2 - \omega_2 D_1}\right]
\end{equation}
Then, using the relations
\begin{align*}
A_2\sin\varphi_2 &= -A_1\sin\varphi_1 - \frac{M_s}{C_1}\\
A_2\cos\varphi_2 &= -A_1\cos\varphi_1
\end{align*}
we have
\begin{align*}
A_2\sin\varphi_2 &= \frac{M_s}{C_1}\left[
  \frac{\omega_1^{2} + D_1^{2} - \omega_1\omega_2 - D_1 D_2}
       {2(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2) - \omega_1^{2} - D_1^{2} - \omega_2^{2} - D_2^{2}}
  \right]\\
A_2\cos\varphi_2 &= \frac{M_s}{C_1}\left[
  \frac{\omega_2 D_1 - \omega_1 D_2}
       {2(\omega_1\omega_2 + D_1 D_2) - \omega_1^{2} - D_1^{2} - \omega_2^{2} - D_2^{2}}
  \right]
\end{align*}
and therefore, that
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:63b}
\varphi_2 = \arctan\left[
  \frac{\omega_1^{2} + D_1^{2} - \omega_1\omega_2 - D_1 D_2}
       {\omega_2 D_1 - \omega_1 D_2}\right]
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
The values of the initial amplitudes are then obtained from the identities
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:64}
\begin{align}
A_1 &= \frac{A_1\sin\varphi_1}{\sin\varphi_1}\label{ch2:eq:64a}\\
A_2 &= \frac{A_2\sin\varphi_2}{\sin\varphi_2}\label{ch2:eq:64b}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

The special case of zero damping is obtained by setting $D_1 = D_2 = 0$. The
characteristic appearance of this kind of motion should by now be familiar to
you, so I won't bother to illustrate it. The coupled step response of a stable
rocket with nonzero damping is shown in Figure~\ref{ch2:fig:28}. As in
Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.1.2}, the severity of the step response is inversely
proportional to the (positive) magnitude of the corrective moment coefficient.
The desirability of a large corrective moment coefficient is therefore
undiminished by the presence of a nonzero roll rate.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=0.85\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/ch2/fig28.png}
  \caption{Roll-coupled response of a model rocket with finite damping to a
  step of intensity $M_s$ in yaw. Both yawing and pitching oscillations occur;
  the pitching motion, however, decays to zero while the yaw angle eventually
  approaches the value $M_s/C_1$.}
  \label{ch2:fig:28}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Complete Response to Impulse Input}\label{ch2:sec:3.2.3}

Should a rocket which is spinning about its longitudinal axis encounter an
impulsive disturbance acting about its yaw axis and of strength $H$, it may be
verified by an argument similar to the one used in
Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.1.3} that the initial effect of the impulse is
identical to the effect of the same impulse upon the rocket when it is
\emph{not} rolling: an initial yaw rate of value $H/I_L$ arises
instantaneously. The subsequent motion is a roll-coupled characteristic
response governed by equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:51} and~\eqref{ch2:eq:55}
through~\eqref{ch2:eq:59} with initial conditions $\alpha_{X0}$,
$\alpha_{Y0}$, and $\Omega_{Y0}$ all equal to zero and initial condition
$\Omega_{X0}$ equal to $H/I_L$.

The impulse response is thus given by equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:51},
\begin{align*}
\alpha_X &= A_1 e^{-D_1 t}\sin(\omega_1 t + \varphi_1)
  + A_2 e^{-D_2 t}\sin(\omega_2 t + \varphi_2)\\
\alpha_Y &= A_1 e^{-D_1 t}\cos(\omega_1 t + \varphi_1)
  + A_2 e^{-D_2 t}\cos(\omega_2 t + \varphi_2)
\end{align*}
where $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are given by equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:56} and
$D_1$ and $D_2$ are computed according to equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:57}.
Substituting the values of the initial conditions into the equations used in
deriving equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:58a}, we have
\begin{align*}
A_1\sin\varphi_1 &= \frac{H}{I_L}\left[
  \frac{D_1 - D_2}
       {2(D_1 D_2 + \omega_1\omega_2) - D_1^{2} - D_2^{2} - \omega_1^{2} - \omega_2^{2}}
  \right]\\
A_1\cos\varphi_1 &= \frac{H}{I_L}\left[
  \frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}
       {2(D_1 D_2 + \omega_1\omega_2) - D_1^{2} - D_2^{2} - \omega_1^{2} - \omega_2^{2}}
  \right]
\end{align*}
from which we conclude that
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:65}
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:65a}
\varphi_1 = \arctan\left[\frac{D_1 - D_2}{\omega_2 - \omega_1}\right]
\end{equation}
The equations used in deriving~\eqref{ch2:eq:58b} become
\begin{align*}
A_2\sin\varphi_2 &= -A_1\sin\varphi_1\\
A_2\cos\varphi_2 &= -A_1\cos\varphi_1
\end{align*}
and thus yield the result
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:65b}
\varphi_2 = \varphi_1
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
A simplification of notation is then possible, in that the single value
$\varphi = \varphi_1 = \varphi_2$ may be applied to the phase angles of both
modes. The equations for the initial amplitudes thus become
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:66}
\begin{align}
A_1 &= \frac{A_1\sin\varphi}{\sin\varphi}\label{ch2:eq:66a}\\
A_2 &= -A_1\label{ch2:eq:66b}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
so that the initial amplitude of the first mode may be denoted by $A$, that of
the second mode by $(-A)$. The roll-coupled impulse response may then be
written in the simplified form
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:67}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_X &= A\left[e^{-D_1 t}\sin(\omega_1 t + \varphi)
  - e^{-D_2 t}\sin(\omega_2 t + \varphi)\right]\\
\alpha_Y &= A\left[e^{-D_1 t}\cos(\omega_1 t + \varphi)
  - e^{-D_2 t}\cos(\omega_2 t + \varphi)\right]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The coupled impulse response of a representative, statically-stable model
rocket having a finite amount of damping is illustrated in
Figure~\ref{ch2:fig:29}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=0.85\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/ch2/fig29.png}
  \caption{Roll-coupled response of a model rocket with finite damping to an
  impulse of strength $H$ in yaw. Again, both pitching and yawing oscillations
  occur; the yawing motion, however, begins instantaneously with an angular
  velocity of $H/I_L$, while the initial pitch rate is zero.}
  \label{ch2:fig:29}
\end{figure}

Although the analytically explicit forms of the equations governing the
maximum angular displacement experienced in a roll-coupled impulse response
are prohibitively complicated, it will be found that a large value of the
longitudinal moment of inertia $I_L$ is desirable, as it was in the decoupled
case, in order to minimize the severity of the response. This characteristic
of the behavior is (again as before) connected with the inverse dependence of
the initial amplitude terms on $I_L$.

\subsubsection{Steady State Response to Sinusoidal Forcing at the Roll Rate}\label{ch2:sec:3.2.4}

While it is certainly possible for a rocket having a zero roll rate to
experience sinusoidal forcing of the various types described in
Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.1.4}, it turns out that the vast majority of cases of
sinusoidal motion encountered in practice are due to causes arising from the
spinning of the rocket itself. Aerodynamic and inertial asymmetries and thrust
misalignments, which appear as step disturbances to non-rolling rockets,
become to a rolling rocket sinusoidal forcing functions whose angular
frequency is identical to the rocket's roll rate. For this reason the
roll-coupled pitch- and yaw-dynamics of cylindrical projectiles under
sinusoidal forcing are usually analyzed only for the case
$\omega_f = \omega_Z$. I shall adhere to this convention and treat, in the
remainder of this section, the particular response of a rolling rocket to
forcing functions of the form
\begin{align*}
f_x(t) &= A_f\sin\omega_Z t\\
f_y(t) &= A_f\cos\omega_Z t
\end{align*}

The dynamical equations that must be solved become, in this case,
\begin{align*}
I_L\frac{d^{2}\alpha_X}{dt^{2}} + C_2\frac{d\alpha_X}{dt} + C_1\alpha_X
  + I_R\omega_Z\frac{d\alpha_Y}{dt} &= A_f\sin\omega_Z t\\
I_L\frac{d^{2}\alpha_Y}{dt^{2}} + C_2\frac{d\alpha_Y}{dt} + C_1\alpha_Y
  - I_R\omega_Z\frac{d\alpha_X}{dt} &= A_f\cos\omega_Z t
\end{align*}
The particular solution to these equations is known to be of the form
\begin{align*}
\alpha_X &= A_r\sin(\omega_Z t + \varphi)\\
\alpha_Y &= A_r\cos(\omega_Z t + \varphi)
\end{align*}
where $A_r$ and $\varphi$ are to be determined by substitution, as in
Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.1.4}. The time derivatives of these expressions are
\begin{align*}
\frac{d\alpha_X}{dt} &= A_r\omega_Z\cos(\omega_Z t + \varphi)\\
\frac{d^{2}\alpha_X}{dt^{2}} &= -A_r\omega_Z^{2}\sin(\omega_Z t + \varphi)\\
\frac{d\alpha_Y}{dt} &= -A_r\omega_Z\sin(\omega_Z t + \varphi)\\
\frac{d^{2}\alpha_Y}{dt^{2}} &= -A_r\omega_Z^{2}\cos(\omega_Z t + \varphi)
\end{align*}
Substituting these into the dynamical equations yields the following set of
algebraic equations:
\begin{align*}
&{-}I_L A_r\omega_Z^{2}\sin(\omega_Z t + \varphi)
  + C_2 A_r\omega_Z\cos(\omega_Z t + \varphi)
  + C_1 A_r\sin(\omega_Z t + \varphi)\\
&\quad - I_R A_r\omega_Z^{2}\sin(\omega_Z t + \varphi) = A_f\sin\omega_Z t\\[1ex]
&{-}I_L A_r\omega_Z^{2}\cos(\omega_Z t + \varphi)
  - C_2 A_r\omega_Z\sin(\omega_Z t + \varphi)
  + C_1 A_r\cos(\omega_Z t + \varphi)\\
&\quad - I_R A_r\omega_Z^{2}\cos(\omega_Z t + \varphi) = A_f\cos\omega_Z t
\end{align*}
Making use of the trigonometric identities
\begin{align*}
\sin(\omega_Z t + \varphi) &= \sin\omega_Z t\cos\varphi + \cos\omega_Z t\sin\varphi\\
\cos(\omega_Z t + \varphi) &= \cos\omega_Z t\cos\varphi - \sin\omega_Z t\sin\varphi
\end{align*}
we can obtain
\begin{align*}
&A_r\left[C_1 - \omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R)\right]
  \left[\sin\omega_Z t\cos\varphi + \cos\omega_Z t\sin\varphi\right]\\
&\quad + C_2 A_r\omega_Z\left[\cos\omega_Z t\cos\varphi - \sin\omega_Z t\sin\varphi\right]
  = A_f\sin\omega_Z t\\[1ex]
&A_r\left[C_1 - \omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R)\right]
  \left[\cos\omega_Z t\cos\varphi - \sin\omega_Z t\sin\varphi\right]\\
&\quad - C_2 A_r\omega_Z\left[\sin\omega_Z t\cos\varphi + \cos\omega_Z t\sin\varphi\right]
  = A_f\cos\omega_Z t
\end{align*}
The terms containing $\sin\omega_Z t$ and those containing $\cos\omega_Z t$
must be independent of each other. We then have, from the first relation
above,
\begin{align*}
A_r\cos\varphi\left[C_1 - \omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R)\right]
  - A_r\sin\varphi\, C_2\omega_Z &= A_f\\
A_r\sin\varphi\left[C_1 - \omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R)\right]
  + A_r\cos\varphi\, C_2\omega_Z &= 0
\end{align*}
From the second equation we see that
\begin{align*}
A_r\cos\varphi\left[C_1 - \omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R)\right]
  - A_r\sin\varphi\, C_2\omega_Z &= A_f\\
-A_r\sin\varphi\left[C_1 - \omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R)\right]
  - A_r\cos\varphi\, C_2\omega_Z &= 0
\end{align*}
The two sets of equations are entirely equivalent; either pair can be chosen
for determining $A_r$ and $\varphi$. Suppose I choose the first pair. The
second member of this pair gives us
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:68}
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:68a}
\varphi = \arctan\left[\frac{C_2\omega_Z}{\omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R) - C_1}\right]
\end{equation}
The first member, when divided by $\cos\varphi$, becomes
\begin{equation*}
A_r\left[C_1 - \omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R)\right]
  - A_r\left[\frac{C_2^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}{\omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R) - C_1}\right]
  = A_f\sec\varphi
\end{equation*}
which, upon substitution of the trigonometric identity
\begin{equation*}
\sec\varphi = \sqrt{\tan^{2}\varphi + 1}
\end{equation*}
may be written in the form
\begin{equation*}
A_r\left[C_1 - \omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R)
  - \frac{C_2^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}{\omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R) - C_1}\right]
  = A_f\sqrt{\frac{C_2^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}{\left[\omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R) - C_1\right]^{2}} + 1}
\end{equation*}
This can readily be transformed into
\begin{equation*}
A_r\left\{\left[\omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R) - C_1\right]^{2} + C_2^{2}\omega_Z^{2}\right\}
  = A_f\sqrt{\left[\omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R) - C_1\right]^{2} + C_2^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}
\end{equation*}
from which we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:68b}
A_r = \frac{A_f}{\sqrt{\left[\omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R) - C_1\right]^{2} + C_2^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
Equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:68} are precisely analogous to
equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:45} (Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.1.4}); the only
difference between the two pairs of formulae is that the quantity $I_L$ in
equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:45} has been replaced by $(I_L + I_R)$ in
equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:68}. We can therefore conduct the remainder of the
present analysis exactly as we did in the case of the non-rolling rocket,
with the aid of a few definitions:
\begin{align}
\omega_{nc} &= \sqrt{\frac{C_1}{I_L + I_R}}\label{ch2:eq:69}\\
\zeta_c &= \frac{C_2}{2\sqrt{C_1(I_L + I_R)}}\label{ch2:eq:70}\\
\beta_c &= \frac{\omega_Z}{\omega_{nc}}\label{ch2:eq:71}\\
\mathit{AR}_c &= \frac{A_r}{A_f}\label{ch2:eq:72}
\end{align}
It is important to remember, however, that the quantities defined by
equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:69} and~\eqref{ch2:eq:70} are highly artificial
constructions created solely for the purpose of analytical convenience. The
\emph{effective natural frequency} $\omega_{nc}$ does not correspond to any
real, physical angular frequency at which the rocket can be expected to
oscillate if undamped; neither does the \emph{effective damping ratio}
$\zeta_c$ bear any relation to any damping parameter we can compute from the
characteristics of the homogeneous response, as was the case for non-rolling
rockets. Both $\omega_{nc}$ and $\zeta_c$ are excellent analytical tools,
though, since they give us a maximum amount of information about the rocket
with a minimum of writing, so you shouldn't let their somewhat tenuous
physical bases bother you too much.

Using equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:69} through~\eqref{ch2:eq:72} we can write, by
analogy with equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:46} through~\eqref{ch2:eq:48}
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:73}
\begin{align}
\varphi &= \arctan\left[\frac{2\zeta_c\beta_c}{\beta_c^{2} - 1}\right]\label{ch2:eq:73a}\\
\mathit{AR}_c &= \frac{1}{C_1\sqrt{(\beta_c^{2} - 1)^{2} + (2\zeta_c\beta_c)^{2}}}\label{ch2:eq:73b}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
and, for the roll coupled resonant frequency and resonance peak,
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:74}
\begin{align}
\beta_{\mathrm{cres}} &= \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta_c^{2}}\label{ch2:eq:74a}\\
\omega_{\mathrm{cres}} &= \beta_{\mathrm{cres}}\,\omega_{nc}\label{ch2:eq:74b}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:75}
\mathit{AR}_{\mathrm{cres}} = \frac{1}{2C_1\zeta_c\sqrt{1 - \zeta_c^{2}}}
\end{equation}
Figures~\ref{ch2:fig:30} and~\ref{ch2:fig:31} illustrate the variation in
$\varphi$ and $\mathit{AR}_c$ with $\beta_c$ for a number of different values of
$\zeta_c$. These functions are, of course, identical to those illustrated in
Figures~\ref{ch2:fig:24} and~\ref{ch2:fig:25}.

All the dangers inherent in decoupled resonant responses are present also in
the roll-coupled case, all the more so since the presence of a roll rate
decreases the effective damping and makes the resonance peak of any given
rocket more severe. In rockets that are not slender enough for $I_R$ to be
negligible compared to $I_L$ the difference between the decoupled and coupled
resonant responses can be significant. The effect of spinning upon a short,
stubby rocket can be truly startling; it can even be serious enough to render
unacceptable a design whose behavior might otherwise be within tolerable
limits.

From our analysis of the roll-coupled sinusoidal steady state we thus conclude
that a large \emph{slenderness ratio} in the sense of a large ratio of $I_L$
to $I_R$, as well as in the sense of a large value of $I_L$ itself, is to be
ranked along with a large corrective moment coefficient and an adequate
damping coefficient as a design feature useful in minimizing the hazards of
resonance.

\subsubsection{Roll Stabilization}\label{ch2:sec:3.2.5}

Since the early days of model rocketry it has been known that the flight path
of a model which has an insufficient, or even slightly negative, static
stability margin can be rendered reasonably straight and true by inducing a
very rapid roll rate in the vehicle at launch. This technique generally goes
by the name of \emph{spin stabilization} or \emph{roll stabilization} and it
is widely believed that the presence of a rapid enough roll rate actually
induces positive aerodynamic stability in an otherwise unstable rocket. As an
examination of the relevant dynamical equations will reveal, it does nothing
of the kind.

First of all, we have our results from Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.2.1} which
indicate that the angle of deflection of a statically-unstable rocket from its
intended direction of flight increases with time after the rocket is
disturbed, just as it does for a statically-unstable rocket which is not
rolling at all. The only case in which the presence of a finite roll rate
prevents an unstable rocket from diverging is that of zero damping
(equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:60}), in which negative values of $C_1$ up to, but
not including, $-\frac{I_R^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}{4I_L}$ can be tolerated while an
oscillation which neither grows nor decays with time is maintained. What
happens when $C_1$ reaches the critical value
$-\frac{I_R^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}{4I_L}$? Well, if $C_1$ is precisely
$-\frac{I_R^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}{4I_L}$ the solution to the dynamical equations
assumes the form
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:76}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_X &= A_1\sin(\omega_c t + \varphi_1) + A_2 t\sin(\omega_c t + \varphi_2)\\
\alpha_Y &= A_1\cos(\omega_c t + \varphi_1) + A_2 t\cos(\omega_c t + \varphi_2)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_c$, the \emph{critical frequency}, is given by
\begin{equation*}
\omega_c = -\frac{I_R}{2I_L}\,\omega_Z
\end{equation*}
Because equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:76} constitute a freak case which is of no
interest whatsoever from the standpoint of design, I will not go into any
detailed derivations of its properties. Suffice it to say that
equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:76} describe a response which grows with time and is
thus unstable.

Should $C_1$ have a larger negative value than
$-\frac{I_R^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}{4I_L}$ a motion of the form
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:77}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_X &= A_1 e^{-\frac{t}{\tau_1}}\sin(\omega_c t + \varphi_1)
  + A_2 e^{-\frac{t}{\tau_2}}\sin(\omega_c t + \varphi_2)\\
\alpha_Y &= A_1 e^{-\frac{t}{\tau_1}}\cos(\omega_c t + \varphi_1)
  + A_2 e^{-\frac{t}{\tau_2}}\cos(\omega_c t + \varphi_2)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
will be observed where, again, $\omega_c = -\frac{I_R}{2I_L}\omega_Z$. The
values of the time constants are given by
\begin{subequations}\label{ch2:eq:78}
\begin{align}
\tau_1 &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{-\left(\frac{C_1}{I_L}
  + \frac{I_R^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}{4I_L^{2}}\right)}}\label{ch2:eq:78a}\\
\tau_2 &= -\tau_1\label{ch2:eq:78b}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Again, since this is a singular case never encountered in practice there is
no useful purpose served in presenting its full derivation. Its only useful
purpose is to confirm that a divergent dynamical response results from
$C_1 < -\frac{I_R^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}{4I_L}$. We see that this is indeed the
case, as the time constant $\tau_2$ is \emph{negative} and will thus cause the
amplitude of the sinusoidal mode with which it is associated to increase
exponentially with time. I might mention here in passing that the behavior
described by equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:76} and~\eqref{ch2:eq:77}
through~\eqref{ch2:eq:78} is very like the ``running down'' of a toy top or
gyroscope. Indeed, the equations involved are rather similar, so if you have
such a device handy you might want to observe the decay of its motion a couple
of times to get a ``feel'' for what the mathematics is saying.

At any rate, what we have established so far is that in the idealization of
zero damping (which never occurs anyway) a rocket spinning at a rate
$\omega_Z$ is capable of tolerating negative stability up to, but not
including, $C_1 = -\frac{I_R^{2}\omega_Z^{2}}{4I_L}$ without diverging (albeit
without the oscillations \emph{subsiding} either, so that the motion is really
only neutrally stable in the dynamic sense). Beyond this value the
oscillations increase in violence until the rocket flips end for end entirely,
destroying all semblance of a true and predictable flight path. In the
\emph{real} case of finite damping, moreover, \emph{any} negative value of
$C_1$, \emph{no matter how slight}, results in unstable flight. What, then,
does roll stabilization really do?

\emph{It suppresses the growth rate of the instability.} A close look at
equations~\eqref{ch2:eq:57} will help to clarify my meaning here. Both the
inverse time constants $D_1$ and $D_2$ involve a fraction whose
\emph{denominator contains the term} $\frac{I_R\omega_Z}{2I_L}$. This means
that for a large enough radial moment of inertia, a fast enough roll rate, or
both the magnitudes of the inverse time constants will be \emph{very small}.
Thus, even though one of them is negative in cases where $C_1$ is less than
zero, it is only slightly so, meaning that the amplitude of the oscillations,
while it does indeed grow with time, does so at a \emph{much slower rate} than
would be the case if the rocket were spinning more slowly or not spinning at
all. It is therefore entirely possible that, if the rocket is spinning with
sufficient rapidity, the rate at which the deflection of the model from its
intended flight path direction grows after it is once disturbed from
equilibrium will be so slight that no appreciable instability will be evident
during the entire upward flight. Once apex is reached and the recovery system
is activated, of course, the stability question becomes irrelevant. If the
spinning of the rocket is capable of holding it reasonably close to its proper
attitude during the handful of seconds it takes to reach its maximum altitude
it will have accomplished its purpose---and this, as we know from experience,
is in fact the case.

Secondly, the presence of a rapid spin rate causes the \emph{angular momentum}
of the rocket to be appreciable. Angular momentum, like linear momentum, is a
vector quantity which can be resolved into components by the methods of
trigonometry. If this is done we find that the Z-component of a rocket's
angular momentum is just $I_R\omega_Z$; a rocket which is not rolling at all
thus has no Z-component of angular momentum, and in fact if it is flying
straight and true it has no angular momentum at all. Now the effect of any
disturbing moment on the model is to change its angular momentum; if it is a
yaw disturbance it will impart an X-component of angular momentum, while if it
is a pitch disturbance it will give rise to a Y-component. If the rocket is
not spinning at all, the components of angular momentum imparted by
disturbances will constitute \emph{all} the angular momentum the rocket has
after any given disturbance. If it \emph{is} spinning, however, the
disturbance-imparted components of angular momentum are only \emph{added
vectorially} to the Z-component which is already present. What this means is
that \emph{a disturbance of a given strength will have less effect on a
spinning rocket than on one which is not spinning} because the change in
angular momentum it imparts is of less importance to the spinning than to the
non-spinning vehicle. The practical consequence of all this is that the
\emph{initial amplitudes} of the roll-coupled motion due to any given
disturbance will be \emph{less} than those of the decoupled response
associated with that same disturbance (you can prove this to yourself by
taking a representative disturbance and calculating its effect on both a
rolling and a non-rolling rocket). Not only, therefore, does the angular
displacement of a rapidly-spinning, unstable rocket increase very slowly after
a disturbance is encountered, but the initial displacement produced by that
disturbance is very slight.

In addition, the kind of physical phenomena that produce transient
disturbances in model rockets generally tend to be evenly distributed about
the rocket's longitudinal axis. That is, it is just as likely that a step or
impulse will be encountered in one direction as it is that it will be
encountered in any \emph{other} direction. This being the case, it is rather
likely that a rocket having encountered one disturbance will soon encounter
another that cancels its effect---\emph{if} the rocket has not diverged too
far from its intended flight direction by that time. If the rocket is unstable
and is not spinning at all, the first disturbance it encounters will send it
head over heels in a tiny fraction of a second, so this ``averaging'' effect
will be no help at all. If it is spinning rapidly enough, though, it will
still be nearly enough ``on course'' when the second disturbance hits to take
advantage of its ameliorating influence. A rapid roll rate makes a rocket
``sluggish'' in its rotational behavior, allowing it to respond more nearly to
the \emph{time average} of disturbances than to the individual disturbances
themselves---and since the time average is generally much less than any one
individual disturbance, this is greatly to the rocket's advantage.

Finally, the presence of roll turns all disturbances due to imperfections in
the rocket itself into sinusoidal forcing at the roll frequency, as mentioned
in Section~\ref{ch2:sec:3.2.4}. You will soon see how important this is if
you examine equation~\eqref{ch2:eq:68b} and imagine $C_1$ to be a negative
number, for if $C_1$ is negative then $(-C_1)$ must be positive. This means
that the quantity $\left[\omega_Z^{2}(I_L + I_R) - C_1\right]$ will always be
greater than $(-C_1)$ alone, which in turn means that \emph{the amplitude of
the response will decrease uniformly as the roll rate increases from zero to
infinity: an unstable rocket cannot experience resonance}. To impart a rapid
roll rate to an unstable rocket is thus to render the effect of a large class
of disturbances upon it inconsequential.

So we see that the list of benefits available to statically unstable rockets
through the mechanism of roll is impressive after all. The net effect of all
of them taken together produces a condition that \emph{looks} enough and
\emph{works} enough like stability that the use of the term ``roll
stabilization'' is really not altogether unjustified.
