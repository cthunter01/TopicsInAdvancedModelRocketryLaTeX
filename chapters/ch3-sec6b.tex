\subsection{The Datcom Method Applied to the Javelin Rocket}\label{ch3:sec:6.2}

To illustrate the application of the equations presented in the preceding section, I shall
perform the zero-lift drag coefficient calculation for a specific model rocket: the Javelin, a
kit produced by the Centuri Engineering Company, and the same model for which Mercer has published
experimentally-determined information on drag obtained through wind-tunnel tests. The
availability of published data gives us a standard by which to measure the accuracy of the
\emph{Datcom} results. The Javelin, a simple, single-staged vehicle with a tangent-ogive nose, is
shown in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:48}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/v2/ch3/fig48.pdf}
  \caption{The Javelin rocket, produced in kit form by the Centuri Engineering Company of Phoenix,
    Arizona, shown with its stock BC-74 nosecone. All dimensions are given in centimeters.}
  \label{ch3:fig:48}
\end{figure}

I shall begin the calculation by making the following assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item the angle of attack is zero;
  \item the model has no launch lug;
  \item the airspeed is 60 meters/second and $\nu = 1.495 \times 10^{-5}$ meter$^{2}$/second;
  \item the finish is hydraulically smooth at the given velocity;
  \item the flow over the fins is completely laminar;
  \item the flow over the body is completely turbulent; and
  \item the flow does not separate from fins or body, except at the base.
\end{enumerate}
The determination then proceeds as follows:

\medskip\noindent Step~1:\quad forebody drag coefficient $(C_{Df})_b$
\begin{align*}
(C_{Df})_b &= (C_f)_B\left[1 + \frac{60}{(\ell_b/d_m)^3} + 0.0025\,\frac{\ell_b}{d_m}\right]\frac{S_s}{S_m}
\end{align*}
Assume $(C_f)_B$ given by equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:86}:
\begin{align*}
(C_f)_B &= (C_f)_{\mathrm{turb}} = \frac{.074}{(R_\ell)^{1/5}}
\end{align*}
Since $\ell_b = 31.75$ cm.\ $= 0.3175$ meter, the Reynolds number based on body length is
given by
\begin{align*}
R_\ell &= \frac{60 \times .3175}{1.495 \times 10^{-5}} = 1.27 \times 10^{6}
\end{align*}
Then the skin-friction coefficient of the body is
\begin{align*}
(C_f)_B &= .00445
\end{align*}
Since
\begin{align*}
\frac{\ell_b}{d_m} &= \frac{31.75}{1.93} = 16.45
\end{align*}
we obtain
\begin{align*}
\left[1 + \frac{60}{(\ell_b/d_m)^3} + .0025\,\frac{\ell_b}{d_m}\right] &= 1 + .0135 + .0411 \cong 1.055
\end{align*}
The ratio of wetted area to cross-sectional area is
\begin{align*}
\frac{S_s}{S_m} &= \left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{CYL}} + \left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{OGIVE}}
\end{align*}
For the cylindrical body we find from~\eqref{ch3:eq:170}\ednote{The factor 4 of the third member is
added per the supplement's correction to page 445 of the 1973 edition; the 1973 display read
$(S_s/S_m)_{\mathrm{CYL}} = 4\,\ell/d_m = 22.61/1.93 = 46.9$.}
\begin{align*}
\left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{CYL}} &= 4\,\frac{\ell}{d_m} = 4\,\frac{22.61}{1.93} = 46.9
\end{align*}
and for the ogival nose Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:46} or equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:171c} gives
\begin{align*}
\left(\frac{S_s}{S_m}\right)_{\mathrm{OGIVE}} &\cong 2.7\,\frac{\ell}{d_m} = 2.7\,\frac{9.14}{1.93} = 12.8
\end{align*}
So
\begin{align*}
\frac{S_s}{S_m} &= 46.9 + 12.8 = 59.7
\end{align*}
The forebody drag coefficient is thus
\begin{align*}
(C_{Df})_b &= .00445 \times 1.055 \times 59.7 = 0.279
\end{align*}

\medskip\noindent Step~2:\quad base drag coefficient $C_{Db}$
\begin{align*}
C_{Db} &= \frac{0.029(d_b/d_m)^3}{\sqrt{(C_{Df})_b}}
\end{align*}
From Step 1,
\begin{align*}
\sqrt{(C_{Df})_b} &= \sqrt{.279} = .528
\end{align*}
and hence the base drag coefficient is
\begin{align*}
C_{Db} &= \frac{.029}{.528} = .055
\end{align*}

\medskip\noindent Step~3:\quad fin drag coefficient $(\CDo)_F$
\begin{align*}
(\CDo)_F &= 2(C_f)_F\left(1 + 2\,\frac{t}{c}\right)\frac{S_F}{S_m}
\end{align*}
Assume $(C_f)_F$ given by equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:63}:
\begin{align*}
(C_f)_F &= (C_f)_{\mathrm{LAM.}} = \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{R_c}}
\end{align*}
The average fin chord is simply
\begin{align*}
c &= \frac{3.97 + 2.07}{2} = 3.02\un{cm.} = .0302\un{meter}
\end{align*}
Hence the Reynolds number for the fins is
\begin{align*}
R_c &= \frac{60 \times .0302}{1.495 \times 10^{-5}} = 1.21 \times 10^{5}
\end{align*}
which gives
\begin{align*}
(C_f)_F &= \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{1.21 \times 10^{5}}} = .00382
\end{align*}
Fins made of 3/32\textquotedbl{} balsa give $t = 0.238$ cm., so
\begin{align*}
\frac{t}{c} &= \frac{.238}{3.02} = 0.079
\end{align*}
The gross area $\sigma_F$ of a single fin can be determined by resolving the Javelin fin into
rectangles and right triangles, as shown in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:49}b; this method simplifies the
calculation of $\sigma_F$ and, although it results in a slightly larger value than that obtained
by the standard method (shown in Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:49}a), the difference is not very
significant. Following Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:49}b, then,
\begin{align*}
\text{Area of region I} &= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1.90 \times 4.19 = 3.98\un{cm.^{2}}\\
\text{Area of region II} &= 2.07 \times 4.19 = 8.68\un{cm.^{2}}\\
\text{Area of region III} &= 3.21 \times 0.965 = 3.10\un{cm.^{2}}\\[1ex]
\sigma_F &= (3.98 + 8.68 + 3.10)\un{cm.^{2}} = 15.76\un{cm.^{2}}
\end{align*}
Since the Javelin has four fins,
\begin{align*}
S_F &= 4\sigma_F \cong 63\un{cm.^{2}}
\end{align*}
The body frontal area is
\begin{align*}
S_m &= \frac{\pi d_m^2}{4} = \frac{3.14 \times 1.93^2}{4} = 2.92\un{cm.^{2}}
\end{align*}
and hence the fin drag coefficient is
\begin{align*}
(\CDo)_F &= 2 \times .00382 \times 1.158 \times \frac{63}{2.92} = .190
\end{align*}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/v2/ch3/fig49.pdf}
  \caption{Standard and approximate methods of determining the gross fin planform area of the
    Javelin rocket. The approximate method (b) is preferred to the standard method (a) because it
    is more conservative; that is, it results in a larger value of $\sigma_F$.}
  \label{ch3:fig:49}
\end{figure}

Summing the results of steps 1, 2, and 3, we obtain the total zero-lift drag coefficient of the
rocket:
\begin{align*}
(\CDo)_{FB} &= .279 + .055 + .190 = .524
\end{align*}

This value is about 25\% greater than the drag coefficient measured by Mercer for the Javelin
rocket with a Centuri BC-74 nosecone (see Figure~\ref{ch3:fig:31}).

Now suppose we see what happens if I rescind assumption (f) above and replace it with the more
accurate assumption that the flow over the body is partly laminar and partly turbulent, with
transition occurring at the critical Reynolds number $R_{\mathrm{crit}} = 5 \times 10^{5}$.

\medskip\noindent Step~1:\quad forebody drag coefficient $(C_{Df})_b$

\noindent The body skin-friction coefficient will now be given by
equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:101}:
\begin{align*}
(C_f)_B &= \frac{0.074}{(R_\ell)^{1/5}} - \frac{B}{R_\ell}
\end{align*}
From equation~\eqref{ch3:eq:100} we find $B = 1735$. Then, since $R_\ell = 1.27 \times 10^{6}$,
\begin{align*}
(C_f)_B &= .00445 - .00137 = .00308
\end{align*}
The forebody drag coefficient then becomes
\begin{align*}
(C_{Df})_b &= .00308 \times 1.055 \times 59.7 = .194
\end{align*}
which is a reduction of 30.5\% from its previous value of 0.279, or a reduction in the
contribution to the overall drag coefficient by 0.085. This is due to the fact that, at these
Reynolds numbers, the skin-friction coefficient for a laminar boundary layer is considerably
lower than that for a turbulent boundary layer.

\medskip\noindent Step~2:\quad base drag coefficient $C_{Db}$

\noindent Using the new value of $(C_{Df})_b$, we find the base drag coefficient to be
\begin{align*}
C_{Db} &= \frac{0.029}{\sqrt{.194}} = .066
\end{align*}
The base drag coefficient is thus \emph{increased} by 20\%, but since it was a relatively small
quantity to begin with the actual increase in drag coefficient due to this percentage increase is
only 0.011.

\medskip\noindent Step~3:\quad fin drag coefficient $(\CDo)_F$

\noindent Since $R_c$ is less than the critical Reynolds number, laminar flow will be maintained
on the fins. The fin drag coefficient thus remains unchanged from its previous value of 0.190.

Summing the contributions from Steps 1, 2, and 3 we obtain a new value for the total drag
coefficient:
\begin{align*}
(\CDo)_{FB} &= .194 + .066 + .190 = .450
\end{align*}

This result is only about 7\% greater than Mercer's measured value of 0.42 for the Javelin
rocket. Actually, we have no right to expect any closer agreement with Mercer's data, both
because a 7\% error is within the measurement uncertainty of the type of equipment used in the
wind-tunnel experiments and because the wind-tunnel tests were conducted at a much lower velocity
than 60 meters/second, and hence a much lower Reynolds number than that on which the above
calculations were based. An airspeed of 15 meters/second is representative of the test velocities
produced by the type of wind tunnel used in the Mercer experiments. Suppose we then rescind
assumption (c) above and replace it with the assumption that the airspeed is 15 meters/second.
This gives $R_\ell = 3.175 \times 10^{5}$ and $R_c = 3.02 \times 10^{4}$. Both Reynolds numbers
are subcritical, so the flow will remain entirely laminar over both body and fins.

\medskip\noindent Step~1:\quad forebody drag coefficient $(C_{Df})_b$

\noindent The body skin-friction coefficient is given by\ednote{From here to ``\ldots{} by more
than 44\%'' below, the text is that of the supplement's replacement for page 451 and the top of
page 452 of the 1973 edition. The 1973 value of $(C_f)_B$ does not follow from its formula
($1.328/\sqrt{31.75 \times 10^{4}} = .00236$), and the figures of Steps 1 and 2 and the total were
computed from it. In Step 3 the supplement also takes $R_c = 3.03 \times 10^{4}$
($15 \times .0302/1.495 \times 10^{-5}$), where the text above gives $3.02 \times 10^{4}$. The
1973 text read $(C_f)_B = (C_f)_{\mathrm{LAM.}} = 1.328/\sqrt{31.75 \times 10^{4}} = .00154$;
$(C_{Df})_b = .00154 \times 1.055 \times 59.7 = .097$; ``This represents a decrease of 0.097, or
50\%, from the transition-flow value of $(C_{Df})_b$.''; $C_{Db} = .029/\sqrt{.097} = .093$; ``This
is an \emph{increase} of 0.028, or 41\% over the transition-flow calculated value.'';
$(C_f)_F = 1.328/\sqrt{3.02 \times 10^{4}} = .00765$;
$(\CDo)_F = 2 \times .00765 \times 1.158 \times 63/2.92 = .380$; ``This is an increase of .190, or
\emph{double} the value calculated for 60 meters/second.''; $(\CDo)_{FB} = .097 + .093 + .380 =
.570$; and ``This is a substantial disagreement with the experimental result---almost 36\%.'' The
other sentences of the passage are unchanged.}
\begin{align*}
(C_f)_B &= (C_f)_{\mathrm{LAM.}} = \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{31.75 \times 10^{4}}} = .00236
\end{align*}
so the forebody drag coefficient is
\begin{align*}
(C_{Df})_b &= .00236 \times 1.055 \times 59.7 = .149
\end{align*}
This represents a decrease of 0.045, or 23.2\%, from the transition-flow calculated value of
$(C_{Df})_b$.

\medskip\noindent Step~2:\quad base drag coefficient $C_{Db}$
\begin{align*}
C_{Db} &= \frac{.029}{\sqrt{.149}} = .075
\end{align*}
This is an \emph{increase} of 0.009, or 13.6\% over the transition-flow calculated value.

\medskip\noindent Step~3:\quad fin drag coefficient $(\CDo)_F$

\noindent The fin skin-friction coefficient is
\begin{align*}
(C_f)_F &= \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{3.03 \times 10^{4}}} = .00763
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{align*}
(\CDo)_F &= 2 \times .00763 \times 1.158 \times \frac{63}{2.92} = .381
\end{align*}
This is an increase of 0.191, or \emph{more than double} the value calculated for 60
meters/second.

From these calculations we obtain an overall drag coefficient of
\begin{align*}
(\CDo)_{FB} &= .149 + .075 + .381 = .605
\end{align*}

This is a substantial disagreement with the experimental result---the calculated value exceeds the
measured value by more than 44\%. And yet the
Mercer data should be \emph{closer} to this value than to the other two values calculated,
according to theory. Discrepancies of this kind can and often do arise, however, due to the
airflow characteristics present in the test sections of many small, subsonic wind tunnels. Test
facilities of this type are prone to have much more \emph{free-stream turbulence} in the air
moving through their test sections than is present in the open atmosphere. The effect of
free-stream turbulence in wind-tunnel testing is to raise the \emph{effective Reynolds number} of
the test; i.e., to make the data look as if the Reynolds number were much higher than it actually
is. One may thus conjecture that, although the Mercer tests may have been conducted at an airspeed
closer to 15 than to 60 meters/second, free-stream turbulence in the wind tunnel made the drag
coefficient appear as if the Reynolds number of the test had been closer to that produced by a 60
meter/second airspeed. If this \emph{were} the case it would present model rocketeers with a
rather paradoxical advantage, for it would mean that a turbulent, low-speed wind tunnel could
produce drag coefficient data applicable to higher-speed model rocket flight in the open air. The
difficulty with such an approach, of course, is that one cannot tell precisely what the effective
Reynolds number of a test in a turbulent wind tunnel is, except by comparing the data with the
semiempirical predictions of \emph{Datcom} theory.

At a given Reynolds number, there are two major parameters which can be used to adjust the
\emph{Datcom} prediction of the drag coefficient of a given rocket: the critical Reynolds number
(and hence, the quantity $B$) and the decrease in base drag coefficient due to the presence of the
fins. Since there are no data available to relate these effects quantitatively to either rocket
geometry or flow conditions, such a process of adjustment must at present be considered pure
guesswork. Variations in critical Reynolds number and base-drag reduction due to the fins could
certainly be considered to account for the discrepancy between the prediction of our last
calculation above and Mercer's experimental determination, but even if numerical estimates of
these quantities are derived which, when used with the \emph{Datcom} method, give an accurate
value of $(\CDo)_{FB}$ for the Javelin rocket, there is no assurance that their use can be
extended to other rocket configurations. The constant $B$, for example, almost certainly depends
upon the individual rocket, so there will always be some error inherent in a general technique
like the \emph{Datcom} method. It can only be hoped that the variation of $B$ from model to model
is small enough so that the error is maintained within acceptable limits for model rocketry
work---say, about 10\%.

In conclusion, it would appear that full adaptation of the \emph{Datcom} method to the practical
calculation of model rocket drag coefficients requires research to establish reasonably
representative values of $B$ for model rockets of different shapes, and to establish a
semiempirical relationship between base drag and fin geometry and location, if this effect is
indeed significant.
